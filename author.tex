% ABSTRACT
% ================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Modern network forensics heavily relies on the reactive reconstruction of digital evidence, such as intrusion detection alerts and system logs. However, digital evidence is fundamentally fragile, as it is prone to accidental loss and vulnerable to deliberate anti-forensic tampering. Traditional Evidence Graphs attempt to reconstruct attack paths purely from observed logs, breaking down completely when evidence is missing. Furthermore, attempting to correlate alerts over legacy static attack graphs ignores the physical reality of evolving network topologies, leading to causality bugs and false positives. In this paper, we introduce the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG), a triage engine that fuses proactive, time-aware structural risk models with reactive forensic evidence. By injecting digital evidence as strict constraints upon the physical topology, F-TAG naturally detects and filters temporal contradictions. As its primary result, when evidence is missing or wiped, F-TAG computes a Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC), which provides a provable bound on unobserved attacker actions under F-TAG's topological assumptions. Secondarily, we explore the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric ($AF_{conf}$), an exploratory Bayesian posterior that leverages granular privilege topologies to probabilistically flag deliberate log tampering. We demonstrate the efficacy of F-TAG on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated enterprise-style topologies up to 100 hosts, successfully recovering complete attack narratives despite severe evidence degradation.

\keywords{Network Forensics \and Temporal Attack Graphs \and Anti-Forensics \and Bayesian Analysis}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

The modern cybersecurity landscape is engaged in a continuous arms race. While network defenses have grown increasingly sophisticated, attackers have similarly evolved, employing advanced persistent threats (APTs) that unfold over weeks or months across complex enterprise topologies~\cite{Niu2016,milajerdi2019holmes}. When an attack is discovered, network forensics becomes the critical task of piecing together the attacker's movements, establishing the scope of the breach, and determining the root cause~\cite{khan2022digital}. This investigative process fundamentally relies on digital evidence, including intrusion detection system (IDS) alerts, firewall logs, and system timestamps.

However, digital evidence is intrinsically fragile. Logs are routinely lost due to benign operational issues, such as buffer overflows, aggressive log rotation policies, and storage failures. Worse still, sophisticated attackers actively engage in anti-forensics by deliberately wiping logs, falsifying timestamps, and clearing event histories to obscure their tracks~\cite{zhang2015detecting}. When critical evidence is missing, traditional reactive forensic methods break down. Legacy approaches, such as Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence}, attempt to reconstruct causal chains using only the observed logs. When a log is wiped, the causal chain shatters, leaving investigators with fragmented, disconnected events and no clear narrative of how an attacker pivoted across the network.

To supplement fragile evidence, researchers have long explored correlating IDS alerts with Attack Graphs, which are proactive models of all possible vulnerability exploitation paths~\cite{1004377,10.1145/586110.586144,Lallie2020}. However, these approaches predominantly utilize static attack graphs, which collapse all network history into a single, timeless map. Attempting to fuse chronological alerts with a timeless graph produces three categories of analytical failures. First, The Dormant Attacker Illusion occurs where attackers blocked by topology are misdiagnosed as intentionally inactive. Second, False-Positive Propagation occurs where alerts are accepted on paths that did not physically exist at the time. Third, Combinatorial Intersection Bugs occur where storing multiple timestamps on a single static node generates impossible backward-time paths during traversal.

To overcome the fragility of reactive evidence and the temporal blindness of static graphs, we introduce the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG). Instead of building graphs out of messy forensic evidence, F-TAG begins with a proactive, mathematical model of the network's shifting physical reality (the Temporal Attack Graph~\cite{Gain2023}) and injects forensic evidence as strict constraints upon it.

This paper makes the following key contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formalize the F-TAG framework that strictly enforces chronological sequencing on observed evidence, naturally filtering false-positive noise and causality bugs through topological contradiction detection.
    \item We introduce the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC), a topology-aware continuous correction that computes provable bounds under F-TAG's execution assumptions on unobserved attacker actions when evidence is missing, maintaining unconditional soundness on both LLDOS benchmarks under the correction's contiguous-execution assumption, which we further characterize under adversarial dormancy in Section~\ref{sec:adversarial-dormancy}.
    \item We present the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric ($AF_{conf}$) as a secondary, exploratory Bayesian posterior that formally quantifies the likelihood of deliberate log-wiping versus benign loss by exploiting topological privilege distributions.
    \item We provide extensive empirical validation on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated enterprise-style topologies, demonstrating F-TAG's robustness against evidence degradation, calibrating its anti-forensic sensitivity, validating its scalability to 100-host networks, and confirming it detects temporally-dependent falsification scenarios where static methods fail.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the foundational theory. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the F-TAG framework and its metric suite. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} details the experimental architecture. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses operational implications. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper, and Section~\ref{sec:future-work} outlines future work.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Attack Graphs and Alert Correlation}

Attack Graphs form the foundation of proactive security modeling by mapping vulnerability dependencies~\cite{Noel2018,Lallie2020}. Early work pioneered their automated generation~\cite{1004377}, scalable monotonic analysis~\cite{10.1145/586110.586140}, and topological vulnerability assessment~\cite{jajodia2005topological}. To bridge proactive defense with reactive investigation, alert correlation techniques evolved to match IDS alert prerequisites and consequences~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building,1366134}. Researchers subsequently leveraged proactive attack graphs to hypothesize missing alerts and predict future intrusions~\cite{10.1016/j.comcom.2006.04.001,Roschke2010,husak2019survey}. However, these alert projection methods rely on static attack graphs, failing to capture the dynamic evolution of network topologies and enabling causally impossible backward-time traversals when timestamps are matched against a timeless structure.

\subsection{Evidence Graphs and Network Forensics}

Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence,he2016network} structure digital evidence into localized graphs for investigative traversal, but break down when logs are missing. To address this, approaches like those by Liu and Ji~\cite{liu2012using} and Barrere et al.~\cite{barrere2017tracking} utilized proactive attack graphs to hypothesize missing events. Furthermore, methodologies like Zhang et al.'s~\cite{zhang2015detecting} focus on detecting deliberate timestamp falsification. However, these methods primarily rely on state-based and statistical correlation models (e.g., Hidden Markov Models and Bayesian Networks~\cite{Frigault2008,Frigault2017}). When attackers deliberately wipe logs or falsify timestamps to create massive chronological gaps or impossible precedences, the transition matrices of HMMs break down and Bayesian Networks often attempt to rationalize impossible sequences rather than strictly rejecting them. 

F-TAG departs from these probabilistic engines by treating time and topology as strict physical boundaries rather than statistical likelihoods, identifying timestamp falsification instantly via topological contradiction (an \texttt{EMPTY} bounding box).

\subsection{Temporal and Dynamic Network Security}

Recognizing the limitations of static models, recent work has shifted toward time-varying graphs~\cite{Holme,Casteigts2012}, demonstrating that temporal constraints fundamentally alter path structures~\cite{Holme2005,Tang2013}. In the security domain, this led to dynamic network security assessments, including temporal risk variance~\cite{Awan2016}, dynamic attack surface evaluation~\cite{ENOCH2019102448,Enoch2018,Bopche2017}, and time-efficient network hardening~\cite{6263942}. 

Gain and Barik~\cite{Gain2023,gain2025augmenting} formalized Temporal Attack Graph (TAG) metrics to evaluate temporally feasible attack paths and optimize micro-segmentation boundaries. Concurrently, Bhardwaj and Dave~\cite{bhardwaj2024attack} advanced active AI-driven attack prediction and mitigation. F-TAG builds directly upon these proactive temporal foundations, evolving them from forward-looking risk assessment tools into a retrospective forensic engine capable of computing formal bounds on missing evidence and probabilistic confidence in anti-forensic tampering.

\subsection{Research Gap}

As Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison} summarizes, the existing literature exhibits a fundamental architectural divide. On one side, reactive forensic methods (Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence}, alert correlation~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,1366134}) consume digital evidence to reconstruct attack narratives, but they collapse entirely when evidence is missing or falsified, because their graph structure is derived from the evidence itself. On the other side, proactive temporal models (TI-HARM~\cite{ENOCH2019102448}, TAG~\cite{Gain2023}) capture the evolving physical topology, but they perform forward-looking risk assessment without any mechanism to incorporate retrospective forensic observations.

No existing framework bridges this divide. F-TAG addresses this gap by treating the proactive temporal topology as a fixed structural reference frame and injecting reactive forensic evidence as strict filtering constraints upon it, enabling the computation of provable bounds on unobserved actions and the structural detection of falsified timestamps.

\section{Foundational Theory}\label{sec:theory}

The foundation of F-TAG relies on rigorous complex network theory to avoid state-space explosion and causal ambiguity. We formalize the core definitions that underpin the entire framework, building upon the Temporal Attack Graph formalism introduced by Gain and Barik~\cite{Gain2023}.

\subsection{Static Attack Graph Formulation}\label{sec:static-ag}

According to the definition provided in \cite{6263942}, an attack graph, denoted as $G$, is a directed graph $G = (E \cup C, R_{r} \cup R_{i})$. The vertex set $E \cup C$ represents the collection of exploits and security conditions, while the edge set $R_{r} \cup R_{i}$ represents the require and imply relations. Here, $R_{r} \subseteq C \times E$ and $R_{i} \subseteq E \times C$. Exploits are represented as $v(h_{s},h_{d})$, for exploits on local hosts, they are denoted as $v(h)$. 

Security conditions are predicates of the form $c(h_{s}, h_{d})$, which signify the fulfillment of a security-related condition $c$ involving the source host $h_{s}$ and the destination host $h_{d}$. In cases where the condition only pertains to a single host, it is represented as $c(h)$. Examples of security conditions include existence of vulnerabilities on a specific host or connections between two hosts etc.

\subsection{Definition 1: Temporal Attack Graph}\label{sec:def1}

\textbf{Definition 1.} A Temporal Attack Graph $\mathcal{G}^a(t_{min}, t_{max})$ is defined as a sequence of discrete attack graph snapshots $(G_0, G_1, \dots, G_{\tau-1})$, where each snapshot $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$ represents the network's vulnerability topology at time window $t$~\cite{Gain2023}. The node set $V_t$ contains host-state pairs representing machines and their security-relevant states (e.g., \texttt{mill:root\_access}), while the edge set $E_t$ contains exploitation paths that are physically possible at time $t$.

The F-TAG engine executes a \emph{Reconstruction Phase} to collapse these discrete snapshots into a unified mathematical structure where each edge $e$ carries a set of \emph{active windows} $\mathcal{W}(e) \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, \tau-1\}$. This representation captures the physical reality that network edges (vulnerability exploitation paths) appear and disappear over time as configurations change, patches are applied, and firewall rules are modified. The unified representation enables efficient algorithmic traversal without redundant duplication of shared topology across windows.

Formally, the reconstructed F-TAG is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}^a_{unified} = \left(V_{all},\ E_{all},\ \mathcal{W}\right)
\end{equation}
where $V_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} V_t$, $E_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} E_t$, and $\mathcal{W}: E_{all} \to 2^{\{0,\dots,\tau-1\}}$ maps each edge to the set of time windows in which it physically exists.

\subsection{Definition 2: Temporal Attack Path}\label{sec:def2}

\textbf{Definition 2.} A temporal attack path $p^a_{ij}$ from node $i$ to node $j$ is a sequence of hops $\langle (e_0, w_0), (e_1, w_1), \dots, (e_{\eta-1}, w_{\eta-1}) \rangle$ satisfying two constraints~\cite{Gain2023}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Existence Constraint:} Each edge $e_k$ is traversed at window $w_k \in \mathcal{W}(e_k)$, ensuring the exploitation path must physically exist at the time it is used.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Constraint:} The window sequence is strictly non-decreasing: $w_0 \le w_1 \le \dots \le w_{\eta-1}$, ensuring the attacker cannot travel backward in time.
\end{enumerate}

The non-decreasing constraint natively prevents impossible backward-time causal loops, ensuring that every enumerated path represents a physically realizable attack sequence. This is a fundamental advantage over static graphs, where pathfinding algorithms are oblivious to time.

\subsection{Definition 3: Temporal Attack Corridor}\label{sec:tac}

\textbf{Definition 3.} For a temporal attack path $p$, the Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC) is defined as the bounding interval of the window sequence:
\begin{equation}
\text{TAC}(p) = [w_0(p),\ w_{\eta-1}(p)]
\end{equation}


\subsection{Path Enumeration Algorithm}\label{sec:enumeration}

The temporal path enumeration follows a depth-first search with chronological pruning, as described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}. This chronological pruning is strictly required over naive static graph intersection to prevent the generation of "Frankenstein" paths. If one simply intersected all edges that ever existed across all windows into a static structure and then ran a standard reachability algorithm, the engine would frequently stitch together an early edge (e.g., existing only in $W_0$) with a late edge (e.g., existing only in $W_2$) via an intermediate node that was patched in $W_1$. This is enforced at Line 14 by restricting each edge to windows $w \ge w_{min}$ during traversal itself, rather than filtering afterward, which substantially prunes the search space and guarantees that every enumerated path represents a physically viable sequence of attacker movements.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Temporal Path Enumeration ($Q^a_{ij}$)}
\label{alg:enumerate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE F-TAG $\mathcal{G}^a$, source node $i$, target node $j$, max hops $H_{max}$
\ENSURE Set of all valid temporal attack paths $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \emptyset$
\STATE $\text{Stack} \gets \{(i, 0, \langle\rangle)\}$ \COMMENT{(node, min\_window, path)}
\WHILE{Stack $\neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $(n_{curr}, w_{min}, \text{path}) \gets \text{Stack.pop()}$
    \IF{$n_{curr} = j$ \AND $|\text{path}| > 0$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \mathcal{P}_{ij} \cup \{\text{TemporalPath(path)}\}$
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$|\text{path}| \ge H_{max}$}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \FORALL{edges $e$ from $n_{curr}$}
        \FORALL{$w \in \mathcal{W}(e)$ where $w \ge w_{min}$}
            \STATE Stack.push$(e.dst, w, \text{path} \cup \{(e, w)\})$
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Completeness of Path Enumeration.}
We formally prove that Algorithm 1 is complete, i.e., it discovers all valid temporal attack paths without missing any structurally feasible corridors.

\textit{Theorem 1 (Completeness of Temporal Enumeration):} Let $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$ be the set of all paths from $i$ to $j$ returned by Algorithm 1. If there exists a temporal path $p^*$ from $i$ to $j$ satisfying the non-decreasing chronological constraints of Definition 2 with length $|p^*| \le H_{max}$, then $p^* \in \mathcal{P}_{ij}$.

\textit{Proof (by contradiction):}
Assume there exists a valid temporal path $p^* = ((e_1, w_1), (e_2, w_2), \dots, (e_n, w_n))$ from $i$ to $j$ with length $n \le H_{max}$ that is not discovered by the algorithm. Because the underlying static graph is finite, the algorithm performs a Depth-First Search over all incident edges, and revisits are permitted up to the bounded depth $H_{max}$, the only mechanism by which $p^*$ could be excluded is if the chronological filter (Line 12: $w \ge w_{min}$) incorrectly prunes an edge $(e_k, w_k) \in p^*$.

Let $(e_k, w_k)$ be the first edge in $p^*$ that is incorrectly pruned. This implies that the algorithm reached node $e_{k}.src$ with some arrival window constraint $w_{min}$, but evaluated $w_k < w_{min}$, resulting in pruning.
However, because $p^*$ is a valid temporal path, it must satisfy the non-decreasing chronological property: $w_k \ge w_{k-1}$ for all $k$. In the recursive call (Line 16), the algorithm passes $w_{min} = w_{k-1}$. Thus, the algorithm evaluates $w_k < w_{k-1}$.
This directly contradicts the premise that $p^*$ is a valid temporal path where $w_k \ge w_{k-1}$. Therefore, no such edge in a valid path can be pruned by the temporal constraint. Since no edge in $p^*$ is pruned and the search explores all outgoing edges, $p^*$ must be discovered and added to $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$. $\square$

\section{The F-TAG Framework}\label{sec:methodology}

While Section~\ref{sec:theory} formalized the proactive structural theory inherited from prior TAG research, this section introduces the core forensic contribution of this paper: the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG) engine. Building on the architectural inversion discussed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, F-TAG consumes observed forensic evidence (IDS alerts, firewall logs, system timestamps) to produce a unified forensic triage suite.

\subsection{Path-Level Forensic Bounds}\label{sec:path-metrics}

The Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) is the central contribution and headline metric of the F-TAG framework. The remaining metrics in this section support forensic triage around the FWoC by answering four fundamental operational questions: how wide is the window of compromise, how well does the observed evidence align with the physical topology, how many competing incident timelines exist, and how fast did the attacker move?

\subsubsection*{Timeline Bounding}

\textbf{Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC).} Given a temporal path $p$ with $H$ hops and a set of $N$ observed evidence timestamps $\mathcal{E} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N\}$ mapped to time windows, the FWoC is the intersection of the structural corridor with the evidence-bounded interval:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwoc}
\text{FWoC}(p) = \text{TAC}(p) \cap \left[\min(\mathcal{E}) - \delta - \delta_s,\ \max(\mathcal{E}) + \delta + \delta_s\right]
\end{equation}

The parameter $\delta$ is a clock-skew tolerance absorbing NTP drift and timestamp uncertainty. The parameter $\delta_s$ is a topology-aware small-sample correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_s}
\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)
\end{equation}

This correction dynamically widens the bounds when evidence is sparse ($N < H$), compensating for the statistical bias where observed evidence min/max naturally drifts inward as the sample size shrinks. When evidence is complete ($N \ge H$), $\delta_s = 0$ and the FWoC relies purely on the raw evidence bounds.

\textbf{Absolute Worst-Case Boundary of $\delta_s$.}\label{sec:worst-case-bounds} Let $p$ be an attack path with $H$ hops and $N$ evidence points. The maximum unobserved actions total $H - N$. In the worst-case boundary drift scenario, all $H - N$ unobserved actions occurred strictly \textit{before} the first observation $\min(\mathcal{E})$, or \textit{after} the last observation $\max(\mathcal{E})$. Since each action occupies at most one window, expanding the raw evidence bounds by $\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)$ guarantees the FWoC encapsulates the true start and end windows, assuming contiguous execution. If $N \ge H$, no expansion is needed ($\delta_s = 0$).

\textit{Contradiction Detection:} If $\text{FWoC}(p) = \emptyset$, the evidence is temporally inconsistent with the structural corridor. This indicates either a false-positive alert, timestamp falsification, or an incorrect attack hypothesis. This native contradiction detection is impossible in static graph approaches, which have no temporal reference frame against which to validate evidence.

\textbf{Corridor Narrowing Ratio} ($\rho$):
\begin{equation}
\rho(p) = \frac{|\text{FWoC}(p)|}{|\text{TAC}(p)|}
\end{equation}
Values approaching 0 indicate that evidence pins the breach precisely within a narrow time window. Values near 1 indicate that the available evidence adds nothing beyond what the topology alone provides.

\subsubsection*{Evidence Reliability}

\textbf{Path Alignment Score} ($A$): For each hop $(e_k, w_k)$ in a candidate path, we check whether the observed evidence window $w_k^{obs}$ (if any) matches the window used by the hop. The alignment score quantifies how well the observed evidence maps to valid topological windows:
\begin{equation}
A(p) = \frac{|\{k : w_k^{obs} = w_k \text{ or } w_k^{obs} = \text{null}\}|}{|p|}
\end{equation}

High alignment scores indicate that the candidate path is well-supported by the available evidence, while low scores suggest the path may not represent the true attack trajectory.

\subsubsection*{Multi-Path Ambiguity}

\textbf{Corridor Overlap Index} ($\Omega$): A Jaccard-style metric measuring the temporal overlap between two candidate corridors:
\begin{equation}
\Omega(p, q) = \frac{|\text{TAC}(p) \cap \text{TAC}(q)|}{|\text{TAC}(p) \cup \text{TAC}(q)|}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Reconstruction Ambiguity} ($k$): The number of mutually non-overlapping corridor clusters among all candidate paths, computed by merging overlapping corridors. When $k = 1$, there is essentially one unambiguous timeline story. When $k > 1$, there exist $k$ genuinely distinct, mutually exclusive incident timelines that the investigator must disambiguate through additional evidence collection.

\subsubsection*{Attack Tempo}

\textbf{Temporal Propagation Rate} ($v$):
\begin{equation}
v(p) = \frac{|p| - 1}{|\text{TAC}(p)|}
\end{equation}
measured in hops per window. High values indicate a scripted or automated intrusion (many hops per window), while low values suggest a patient, manual operator (few hops spread across many windows).

\textbf{Stage Transition Gaps} ($\Delta_k$): For consecutive hops:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_k = w_{k+1} - w_k
\end{equation}
Large gaps indicate dormancy or staging periods between attack stages, while $\Delta_k = 0$ indicates same-window lateral movement.

\textbf{Dwell Time Bounds} ($DT$): The lower bound is the sum of minimum physical execution times along the path: $DT_{lower} = \sum_k \delta_{t,min}(e_k)$. The upper bound is the TAC width converted to real time: $DT_{upper} = |\text{TAC}(p)| \times W_{duration}$, where $W_{duration}$ is the window duration in seconds.

\subsection{Topological Triage \& Scoping}\label{sec:network-metrics}

While the previous section evaluated individual attack paths, this section aggregates those path-level metrics into a network-wide view. These scoping metrics answer two questions: which hosts represent inescapable investigative chokepoints, and what is the absolute earliest temporal origin of the attack?

\subsubsection*{Host-Level Triage}

\textbf{Temporal Chokepoint Score} ($\chi$): The fraction of evidentially surviving candidate paths that pass through a given host $h$:
\begin{equation}
\chi(h) = \frac{|\{p \in \mathcal{P}_{surviving} : h \in V(p)\}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}|}
\end{equation}

This generates a prioritized hit-list of hosts for the investigator to examine, ranked by their criticality to the specific incident rather than abstract global topological importance. A host with $\chi = 1.0$ lies on every surviving candidate path and must have been traversed by the attacker.

\textbf{Host Exposure Window}: The set of time windows during which a host was both vulnerable and reachable:
\begin{equation}
W_{exposure}(h) = \bigcup_{e \in E_{all}: h \in \{e.src, e.dst\}} \mathcal{W}(e)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Investigation Scoping}

\textbf{Temporal Search Radius} ($T_{min}$): The earliest window across all backward-reachable paths to the victim node $j^*$:
\begin{equation}
T_{min}(j^*) = \min_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{*j^*}} w_0(p)
\end{equation}

This provides the absolute earliest formal origin window, allowing analysts to aggressively scope their historical log searches and avoid wasting resources on irrelevant time periods. Crucially, $T_{min}$ is derived purely from topology and is therefore invariant to evidence degradation. Even when 75\% of evidence is missing, $T_{min}$ remains stable, serving as the ultimate fail-safe anchor for investigation scoping.



\subsection{Anti-Forensics Confidence ($AF_{conf}$)}\label{sec:afconf}

To probabilistically detect deliberate log tampering, we formulate the Anti-Forensics Confidence as a Bayesian posterior probability. While $AF_{conf}$ is a secondary, exploratory metric compared to the deterministic bounds of the FWoC, it warrants rigorous validation because its primary failure mode (false accusations of active anti-forensics) can severely misdirect incident responders and unnecessarily escalate triage severity. This approach extends prior Bayesian attack graph models~\cite{Frigault2017,Kazeminajafabadi2023} from proactive risk assessment to retroactive forensic analysis. For a missing-evidence gap $G$ at node $n$ with observed features $x$ (primarily the privilege level at the node):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:afconf}
AF_{conf}(G) = P(H_1 \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}
\end{equation}
where $H_1$ represents the hypothesis of deliberate tampering and $H_0$ represents the hypothesis of benign evidence loss.

\subsubsection{Calibrating the Likelihoods.}

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_1)$: Deliberate Wiping Likelihood.} Modifying system log files (e.g., \texttt{/var/log/*}, Windows Event Logs, \texttt{auditd} records) intrinsically requires root or administrative privileges. This target-access requirement provides a domain-informed likelihood of $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.75$ for root-privileged nodes and $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.25$ for non-root nodes. This assignment is stronger than tool-launch based approaches (e.g., Liu et al.~\cite{liu2012using}), as it reflects the fundamental OS-level access control model.

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_0)$: Benign Loss Likelihood.} This is grounded directly in the graph topology, calculated as the base rate of root-level nodes across the entire Datalog state space $\mathcal{G}^a$:
\begin{equation}
P(x \mid H_0) = P_{root,H_0} = \frac{|\{n \in V_{all} : \text{priv}(n) = \text{root}\}|}{|V_{all}|}
\end{equation}

This base-rate calibration ensures that $AF_{conf}$ is tailored to the specific topology under investigation, rather than relying on fixed assumptions about network structure.

\subsubsection{Formal Properties.}

\textit{Lemma 1 (Monotonicity under Uniform Base Rates):} Assuming uniform structural base rates across privilege levels, the Anti-Forensics Confidence $AF_{conf}(G)$ is strictly non-decreasing with respect to the attacker's privilege level at the point of evidence loss. 

\textit{Proof:} Let $x_{root}$ and $x_{user}$ be observed features representing root and user-level privileges, respectively. Based on our domain grounding, $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$. If we hold the structural base rate exactly equal ($P(x_{root} \mid H_0) = P(x_{user} \mid H_0) = c$), we can evaluate the ratio of the posteriors:
\begin{equation}
\frac{AF_{conf}(G_{root})}{AF_{conf}(G_{user})} = \frac{ \frac{P(x_{root} \mid H_1)P(H_1)}{P(x_{root} \mid H_1)P(H_1) + c P(H_0)} }{ \frac{P(x_{user} \mid H_1)P(H_1)}{P(x_{user} \mid H_1)P(H_1) + c P(H_0)} }
\end{equation}
Since $f(y) = \frac{a y}{a y + b}$ is a monotonically increasing function for $a, b, y > 0$, and since $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$, it strictly follows that $AF_{conf}(G_{root}) > AF_{conf}(G_{user})$. This monotonic behavior maps the real-world operational requirement of log-wiping capability directly to the metric's discriminative power under balanced topologies. $\square$

\textit{Boundary Condition (Root Saturation Inversion):} The assumption of a constant base rate breaks down in heavily root-privileged networks. If a topology is nearly saturated with root nodes ($p \to 1$, so $P(x_{root} \mid H_0) \to 1$), the base rate of the rare non-root nodes approaches zero ($P(x_{user} \mid H_0) \to 0$). In this extreme regime, the posterior for a missing gap at a non-root node evaluates to $AF_{conf}(G_{user}) \to 1.0$, artificially spiking higher than the root node's score and inverting the monotonicity.

While the boundary condition above shows non-root posteriors can spike under saturation, the root-node posterior itself remains well-behaved: its limiting value stays strictly below the prior, as we show next.

\textit{Lemma 2 (Root-Node Posterior Limit under Saturation):} The theoretical upper limit of $AF_{conf}(G)$ is bounded by the topological saturation of the network, dropping strictly below the prior under the model's parameter setting.

\textit{Proof:} We analyze the limit behavior of $AF_{conf}(G)$ as the network becomes saturated with root-level nodes. Let the proportion of root nodes approach 1, implying $P(x \mid H_0) \to 1$. Because root access is required for tampering, we set $P(x \mid H_1) = \alpha$, where the operational model fixes $\alpha = 0.75$. 
The posterior limit is:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{P(x \mid H_0) \to 1} AF_{conf}(G) = \frac{0.75 P(H_1)}{0.75 P(H_1) + 1 \cdot P(H_0)}
\end{equation}
Because $P(H_1) < 1$ and $P(H_0) > 0$, this limit strictly evaluates to a value less than the prior $P(H_1)$ (e.g., for $P(H_1)=0.15$, the limit is $\approx 0.117$). Only if we assume an extreme theoretical case where adversarial wiping \textit{always} occurs at root nodes ($\alpha \to 1$) does the limit approach the prior $P(H_1)$. $\square$

\section{Experimental Architecture}\label{sec:experiments}

To rigorously validate the F-TAG framework, we employ two complementary experimental configurations and one Bayesian calibration suite. All experiments were implemented in Python 3.10 using the NumPy and scikit-learn libraries. The complete experimental pipeline comprises approximately 3,500 lines of code across 10 modules. To ensure experimental clarity and avoid conflating dataset limitations, the validation scope is strictly separated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DARPA LLDOS (1.0 and 2.0.2)} validates: temporal reconstruction, missing evidence handling, attack-path recovery, forensic window bounding, and temporal chokepoint behavior.
    \item \textbf{MulVAL} validates: temporal topology construction, privilege-aware $AF_{conf}$ behavior, scalability characteristics, and falsification scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ground Truth Validation (DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2)}\label{sec:tracka}

We validated the core forensic metrics against the DARPA 2000 LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 datasets~\cite{1366134}. These represent historically verified benchmarks for multi-stage intrusions, providing two distinct structural scenarios. LLDOS 1.0 is a highly noisy, distributed DDoS campaign where the attacker pivots through a master host to compromise three parallel zombie hosts (Mill, Pascal, and Locke). Conversely, LLDOS 2.0.2 is a stealthy, rigidly serialized exfiltration attack across identical network boundaries.

\subsubsection{Parser and Graph Construction.} We built a custom parser (\texttt{lldos\_tag\_builder.py}, 700 lines) to strip away the massive background noise of the DARPA network, isolating the verified causal capability paths for both campaign scenarios:
\begin{align*}
\text{LLDOS 1.0 (Distributed DDoS):} & \quad \text{Attacker} \to \text{mill} \to \{ \text{pascal}, \text{locke} \}\text{, with mill, pascal, and locke each independently reaching } \text{ddos\_target} \\
\text{LLDOS 2.0.2 (Linear Exfil):} & \quad \text{Attacker} \to \text{mill} \to \text{pascal} \to \text{mail\_exfil}
\end{align*}

The parser maps the ground-truth attack stages to temporal windows based on the known attack timeline, constructs the F-TAG with appropriate edge active windows, and injects evidence observations as window-mapped timestamps.

\subsubsection{Degradation Sweep Protocol.} We executed a 100-trial Monte Carlo simulation on both datasets at each of four completeness levels (100\%, 75\%, 50\%, 25\%). At each trial, evidence is randomly dropped according to the completeness percentage, and all six forensic metric tiers are computed on the degraded evidence set. This protocol tests the robustness of every metric under progressively severe evidence loss across two independent attack topologies.

\subsubsection{Small-Sample Correction.} Under severe degradation, the raw FWoC suffers from a textbook statistical small-sample bias: the observed minimum and maximum evidence naturally drift inward as the sample size shrinks, artificially collapsing the bounding box and destroying soundness (the probability that the true attack falls within the computed bounds). Our topology-aware correction $\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:delta_s}) dynamically widens the bounds based on the worst-case unobserved path length, producing bounded, monotonic structural scaling rather than unpredictable threshold collapses.

To prove the mathematical generality of $\delta_s$ beyond the benchmark $H=5$, we evaluated the continuous formula against a naive discrete ratio across varying path lengths. While naive scaling dangerously under-approximates long unobserved chains (e.g., allocating only a 1-window expansion for 5 missing steps when $H=10$), our continuous bound $\delta_s = H - N$ strictly preserves the worst-case sequential duration regardless of scale.

\subsection{Scalable Topology Generation (MulVAL)}\label{sec:trackb}

To demonstrate that F-TAG scales to modern enterprise topologies with complex adversarial tactics, we employed the MulVAL logic engine~\cite{mulval}, an industry-standard tool that generates attack graphs from Datalog specifications of network configurations and vulnerability databases.

\subsubsection{Temporal Generation Engine and Formal Datalog Mapping.} We addressed the absence of dedicated temporal attack graph generation tools by wrapping MulVAL in an automated temporal simulator. The engine dynamically generates sequential Datalog configurations representing the network at different time windows, injects vulnerabilities across specific windows (simulating patch cycles and configuration changes), and runs the Docker-containerized MulVAL iteratively to compile the unified F-TAG from the resulting snapshots.

To rigorously bind the static MulVAL output to the temporal domain, we define a mapping function $\phi_{temporal}$ that injects chronological constraints into the generated Datalog facts. Let $f_{static}$ be a standard MulVAL fact, such as \texttt{execCode(host, priv)}. Our engine transforms this into a temporally constrained fact:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{temporal}(f_{static}, w) = \texttt{execCode(host, priv)} \wedge \text{active}(w)
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the specific temporal window during which the underlying network configuration (e.g., firewall rule, unpatched vulnerability) exists.

For example, a static interaction rule in MulVAL (e.g., \texttt{execCode(H, P) :- vulExists(H, V), netAccess(A, H)}) is converted by our engine, which automatically maps the existence of the vulnerability \texttt{vulExists} and the network access \texttt{netAccess} to a subset of windows $\mathcal{W}_{active} \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, \tau\}$. When the temporal graph is compiled, the resulting edge $E = (\text{Attacker}, \text{Host})$ inherits its active window set from the union of the times these prerequisites are satisfied across the simulation sequence (as implemented via the accumulator in the generative engine):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{W}(E) = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau} \{t : \texttt{vulExists}_t \wedge \texttt{netAccess}_t\}
\end{equation}
This formal translation ensures that F-TAG's topological edges strictly adhere to physical reality at any given time $w$, automatically resolving impossible static intersections before forensic triage even begins.

\subsubsection{Causal Reversal Verification.} MulVAL natively outputs Goal $\to$ Precondition dependency edges (reflecting the logical derivation order). Our engine automatically reverses these to represent chronologically accurate Attacker $\to$ Goal causal pathways. We empirically verified that temporal constraints are preserved during this transformation by tracing individual edge reversals. For example, MulVAL Edge $4 \to 5$ in Window 2 correctly reverses to an F-TAG Edge between the corresponding hashed node identifiers while preserving its $\{2, 3, 4\}$ active windows set.

\subsubsection{Adversarial Dormancy Topology.}\label{sec:adversarial-dormancy} To empirically test the boundary condition of contiguous execution (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}), we generated a specialized 5-hop linear chain topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) via MulVAL. We extended the active vulnerability window across 21 windows (W8 to W28) and explicitly instantiated a dormant adversarial path: an early cluster of reconnaissance hops, an 18-window period of dormancy, and a final cluster of exfiltration hops. For this synthetic scenario, the \textit{true compromise interval} was defined as the span from the initial pivot establishment through the final exfiltration (W9 to W28), strictly mirroring the ``exploit through launch'' convention established for the LLDOS datasets.

\subsubsection{Static vs. Temporal Comparison.} Using a 6-node linear chain topology (built with the same generator used for the adversarial dormancy experiments introduced above, here configured with a narrower W8--W13 active window), we ran a direct comparison (\texttt{run\_static\_vs\_temporal\_mulval.py}) between a traditional static attack graph and our temporal attack graph. The topology restricts vulnerabilities to a 6-window span (W8--W13) across 20 total time windows. The static graph identified exactly 1 structural path to the target. The F-TAG expanded this into \textbf{252 distinct chronological corridors} (representing every valid temporal instantiation of the 5 attacker hops across the active windows), accurately mapping how the attack could span across time (Fig.~\ref{fig:static-vs-tag}).

\subsubsection{False-Positive Filtering Experiment.} We also ran a targeted false-positive filtering test to demonstrate how F-TAG handles alerts that violate temporal prerequisites. The full scenario and its results are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-scenarios}.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration of $AF_{conf}$}\label{sec:trackc}

To rigorously calibrate $AF_{conf}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:afconf}), we constructed a large-scale Monte Carlo injection framework (\texttt{run\_af\_calibration.py}). Over 10 random seeds and three prior settings ($P(H_1) \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$), we injected thousands of synthetic missing-evidence gaps into the MulVAL topology (evenly distributed across all 15 candidate paths to ensure a true topological average, $\sim$45 samples per class per seed) and separately into the LLDOS topologies, testing the same three distinct injection classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wipe-Class ($H_1$)}: Deliberate log-wiping biased toward high-privilege pivot nodes, weighted by both root status ($10\times$ weight for root nodes) and temporal chokepoint score $\chi$ (higher-$\chi$ nodes receive proportionally more wipe injections).
    \item \textbf{Random-Benign ($H_0$)}: Uniformly distributed evidence loss across all path nodes, simulating accidental data loss with no structural bias.
    \item \textbf{Rotation-Benign ($H_0$)}: Periodic gaps recurring at consistent nodes, simulating correlated log rotation schedules where the same machines consistently lose logs at regular intervals.
\end{enumerate}

The Rotation-Benign class tests the metric's robustness against structured administrative maintenance. Because periodic rotation affects a small, fixed subset of nodes globally, its intersection with any specific attack path is highly variable. This creates a structurally correlated, uncontrolled sample size of gaps that can either heavily target or completely miss the evaluated path, serving as a high-variance confound distinct from uniform noise.



\subsection{Falsification Detection Benchmark}\label{sec:falsification-exp}

To evaluate F-TAG's ability to detect timestamp falsification, we implemented three adversarial scenarios against the Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2015detecting} static baseline. Note that our baseline implements Zhang et al.'s core precedence-checking principle as a simplified single-pass monotonicity check across the recovered path, rather than their full local per-host adjacency algorithm, which serves as a faithful representation of the static method's temporal capability boundary.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Precedence Swap}: Timestamps of two attack stages are exchanged, violating causal order. Both static and temporal methods can detect this.
    \item \textbf{State Revocation}: A vulnerability is patched (state revoked) between two windows, but the falsified timestamp claims exploitation occurred after the patch. Only temporal methods can detect this, as static methods have no concept of state changes.
    \item \textbf{Window Closure}: An edge ceases to exist in a later window, but the falsified timestamp claims traversal after closure. Again, only temporal methods can detect this.
\end{enumerate}

All experimental code, including the DARPA LLDOS parser, MulVAL temporal simulator, falsification injection framework, and $AF_{conf}$ calibration suite, will be made publicly available upon publication. All Monte Carlo experiments use fixed random seeds (documented per-experiment in the corresponding source files) to ensure exact reproducibility of reported results.

This mathematically proves that F-TAG avoids over-flagging robust attacks (resilience), but flawlessly detects true structural manipulation (detection).

\subsubsection{Baseline Discriminative Power.} Across all three prior settings ($P(H_1) \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$) on the unpruned MulVAL topology, the metric maintained a stable aggregated ROC AUC of 0.801 (95\% CI: [0.797, 0.805]) for Wipe vs. Random classification. \textit{Note on Topological Pruning:} The $AF_{conf}$ calibration was intentionally conducted on the raw, unpruned MulVAL topology. The aggressive host-to-host pruning applied for scalability in Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability} collapses intermediate logical states into uniform high-privilege `execCode` transitions. Because $AF_{conf}$ derives its signal from privilege variance along a path, evaluating it on a fully pruned graph artificially degrades its discriminative power. Thus, calibrating $AF_{conf}$ requires a higher topological granularity than baseline reachability checks.

\subsubsection{Rotation-Benign Variance.} When aggregated across all paths, Wipe vs. Rotation yielded an average AUC of 0.800 (95\% CI: [0.742, 0.858]), effectively identical to the Random baseline. Our diagnostic tracking confirmed that structured periodic rotation lands on root-privilege nodes at a rate mathematically equivalent to uniform noise on average (25.4\% vs. 25.2\%). However, the massive variance in the 95\% CI ([13.9\%, 37.0\%] for Rotation vs [24.6\%, 25.8\%] for Random) perfectly highlights the structural confound: because periodic rotation affects a small fixed subset of nodes globally, its intersection with any single attack path is extremely volatile. Rotation-Benign is not topologically "harder" or "easier" than random noise overall, but it serves as a highly path-dependent confound.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Not Simply Timestamp a Static Graph?}

As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, static graphs fail forensically for three structural reasons (dormant attacker illusion, false-positive propagation, combinatorial intersection bugs); Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}'s chronological constraint eliminates all three by construction.

\subsection{F-TAG vs. Evidence Graphs}

Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence} build narratives \textit{bottom-up} from observed logs. F-TAG operates \textit{top-down}, deriving structure from physical topology and injecting evidence as filters. This architectural inversion provides resilience to missing evidence (retaining structural bounds via FWoC), native contradiction detection against false-positive alerts, and a guaranteed lower bound ($T_{min}$) that holds regardless of how much evidence is lost.

\subsection{Algorithmic Complexity and Scalability}

F-TAG's computational cost is divided into proactive generation (executed offline) and reactive triage. During triage, temporal path enumeration is bounded by $O(k_{max}^{H_{max}})$, but the chronological constraint forces early termination, keeping the effective out-degree small. Once candidate paths are enumerated, metric computation (FWoC, $\chi$, $AF_{conf}$) requires at most $O(|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}| \cdot H_{max})$ time. For highly dense topologies, F-TAG natively supports Hierarchical Decomposition~\cite{Hong2016} across subnet boundaries.

\subsection{Operational Deployment}

F-TAG integrates natively into existing SOC workflows~\cite{khan2022digital}. Structural TAG generation runs proactively during vulnerability cycles. Upon incident detection, the reactive engine instantly fuses SIEM alerts to compute the FWoC bound, prioritize investigative hosts ($\chi$), flag anti-forensic tampering ($AF_{conf}$), and characterize attack tempo ($v$).

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

While F-TAG provides rigorous structural bounds on forensic data, several limitations constrain its current operational generalizability. First, our anti-forensics ground truth and $AF_{conf}$ discriminative thresholds were validated primarily on synthetic topologies (MulVAL), making live SIEM data verification across diverse enterprise architectures a necessary future step. Second, the $AF_{conf}$ model relies on a manually grounded, binary privilege distribution (root vs. non-root), which limits its discriminative power compared to granular access control models. Finally, while computational cost scales predictably, the combinatorial edge density in highly dense networks requires active hierarchical decomposition to maintain real-time triage performance.

The worst-case $\delta_s$ correction (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}) is proven under an assumption of contiguous execution---that unobserved hops occur at approximately one window of drift each. Under this assumption, soundness (the probability the true compromise falls within the computed FWoC) holds unconditionally, as confirmed empirically on both LLDOS datasets (100\% across all completeness levels). We stress-tested this assumption on a synthetic MulVAL topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) modeling a deliberately dormant APT with an 18-window gap between reconnaissance and final action. Under this adversarial dormancy pattern, soundness degrades from 0\% at minimal evidence ($N=1$) to 100\% at full evidence ($N=5$) (see Table~\ref{tab:dormant-apt}), demonstrating that the correction's guarantee is scoped to bounded inter-hop gaps rather than unconditional across arbitrary attacker dormancy. Practitioners investigating suspected long-dormancy campaigns should treat FWoC bounds with reduced confidence when evidence is sparse and gaps between observed evidence points are large relative to path length.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG), a framework fusing proactive structural risk assessment with reactive digital evidence. Our expanded Monte Carlo validation demonstrates that the Forensic Window of Compromise computes provable bounds on unobserved attacker actions, maintaining 100\% soundness across all evidence degradation levels on both real-world LLDOS benchmarks, under the correction's proven contiguous-execution assumption (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}); we further show this guarantee is bounded under deliberate long-dormancy adversarial behavior (Section~\ref{sec:adversarial-dormancy}). The topology-derived $T_{min}$ remains unconditionally stable regardless of evidence loss, serving as a fail-safe investigative anchor. F-TAG successfully detects temporally-dependent falsification scenarios where static methods fail, and the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric achieves a 0.0\% false-accusation rate at the operational threshold of 0.85, pending real-world validation. By shifting from bottom-up evidence reconstruction to top-down structural filtering, F-TAG provides a mathematically grounded approach to network investigation resilient to evidence degradation.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future-work}

The threats to validity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:threats} point directly to future research directions. A primary focus is multi-level privilege modeling (e.g., SELinux contexts) to improve $AF_{conf}$ discriminative power. Additionally, operational deployment necessitates cross-topology generalization testing and calibration against live SIEM data with verified anti-forensic ground truth. Finally, integrating F-TAG directly with live SIEM pipelines would allow the FWoC to dynamically narrow during active incident triage.
