\title{F-TAG: Forensic Temporal Attack Graphs for Evidence-Resilient Network Investigation}

\author{Ayan Gain$^{1}$ \and Mridul Sankar Barik$^{1}$ \\
Jadavpur University, Kolkata, India \\
\texttt{ayan.gain2010@gmail.com, mridulsankar.barik@jadavpuruniversity.in}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Modern network forensics heavily relies on the reactive reconstruction of digital evidence, such as intrusion detection alerts and system logs. However, digital evidence is fundamentally fragile, as it is prone to accidental loss and vulnerable to deliberate anti-forensic tampering. Traditional Evidence Graphs attempt to reconstruct attack paths purely from observed logs, breaking down completely when evidence is missing. Furthermore, attempting to correlate alerts over legacy static attack graphs ignores the physical reality of evolving network topologies, leading to causality bugs and false positives. In this paper, we ask a fundamental question: \emph{Can a temporally evolving attack topology serve as a strict structural constraint for forensic reconstruction when evidence is incomplete or contradictory?} To answer this, we formalize the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG). By injecting digital evidence as constraints upon the physical topology, F-TAG natively detects temporal contradictions. We derive the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) to compute provable bounds on unobserved attacker actions under a contiguous-execution assumption, and we formally characterize the limits of these guarantees under adversarial dormancy. Furthermore, we introduce an Anti-Forensics Confidence ($AF_{conf}$) metric to probabilistically distinguish deliberate log-wiping from routine evidence loss. We demonstrate the efficacy of F-TAG on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated enterprise topologies, successfully recovering complete attack narratives despite severe evidence degradation.

\textbf{Keywords:} Network Forensics, Temporal Attack Graphs, Anti-Forensics, Bayesian Analysis
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Imagine an investigator responding to a breach whose only visible trace is a single IDS alert on the database, the upstream web server's logs are gone. Did the attacker exploit a vulnerability patched last week, or a different one entirely? Was the log wiped on purpose? Without answers, the investigation stalls~\cite{khan2022digital}. Two separate problems are tangled together here: the evidence itself may be missing or falsified, and even the evidence that survives must be checked against whether the attack it implies could ever have actually happened, given how the network changed over time.

Existing tools solve one problem or the other, never both. \emph{Evidence Graphs}~\cite{wang2008evidence} reconstruct causal chains directly from logs and alerts. They work well when evidence is complete, but the chain simply breaks wherever a log is wiped. Conversely, \emph{Static Attack Graphs}~\cite{1004377,Lallie2020} are often used to bridge these gaps by correlating alerts against known vulnerability paths~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building}. While they successfully map every path the network has ever permitted, they collapse that history into a single timeless snapshot, offering no notion of when each path was actually usable.

\textbf{Why a static attack graph is not enough.} A static attack graph only asks about reachability, \emph{``can these vulnerabilities be chained across the network?''}, while forensics must also account for time and order: \emph{``could this sequence of actions have actually happened in this order, given how the network and its vulnerabilities changed over time?''}

For example, suppose a vulnerability is patched on Tuesday. Because a static attack graph does not track time, it still treats that path as open on Wednesday. If an alert fires on Wednesday, the graph will accept an impossible route through a vulnerability that was already closed. This problem becomes even worse across multi-step attacks: without checking whether a path remained open over time, a static graph can easily stitch an exploit from Week~1 to an alert in Week~3 through a server patched in Week~2. The result is an impossible ``Frankenstein path'', an attack narrative assembled from steps that were each valid at different moments in the past, but could never have happened together in reality.

This absence of end-to-end temporal enforcement produces three distinct forensic failure modes. \textbf{Temporal Feasibility Errors} occur when the topology itself is internally inconsistent: an edge existed, but not at the moment the preceding hop required it. \textbf{Topology Transition Errors} occur when an enabling vulnerability disappears mid-attack, between one stage and the next. Unlike the preceding structural errors, \textbf{Evidence Stitching Errors} occur at the forensic evidence layer: individual alerts each match some static edge, but no single chronological sequence can accommodate all of them together. In every case, the underlying cause is the same, the graph has no way to ask whether a \emph{sequence}, not just its individual steps, was ever physically realizable.

Temporal Attack Graphs (TAGs)~\cite{Gain2023} resolve the first two failure modes structurally, by requiring that the window in which each hop is used never precede the window used by the hop before it. This is the key difference from simply stamping timestamps onto static edges: a TAG enforces the ordering constraint \emph{jointly across the entire path during traversal}, not edge by edge after the fact, so a Frankenstein path is never assembled in the first place. What a TAG alone cannot do is the third failure mode: it has no mechanism for taking in observed evidence and checking it against that structure, or for reasoning about what must have happened during a gap where evidence is missing. Forensic investigation needs precisely that, a way to inject retrospective evidence as a constraint on a temporally consistent topology, and to draw sound conclusions when the evidence is incomplete or contradictory.

This is the gap F-TAG closes. The relevant object for forensic correlation is therefore not a static attack path, but a temporally feasible attack corridor constrained by whatever evidence survives. Rather than building the attack graph from the evidence itself, F-TAG first enumerates every temporally feasible attack corridor from the topology alone, then uses observed alerts and timestamps to constrain those corridors retrospectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:motivating-example}). This lets F-TAG reject evidence that is temporally impossible outright, while still bounding the unobserved portions of an otherwise-feasible attack through the Forensic Window of Compromise (see Section~\ref{sec:threats} for a discussion of the limits of these structural bounds under adversarial dormancy).

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Formalizing temporal forensic reconstruction by injecting reactive evidence as strict structural constraints upon proactive, time-aware attack topologies.
    \item Deriving the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) to establish conditional soundness guarantees that bound unobserved attacker actions when evidence is missing.
    \item Demonstrating temporal contradiction detection to natively identify and filter false-positive alerts and falsified evidence where static methods fail.
    \item Characterizing the limits of these structural guarantees under adversarial dormancy, demonstrating exactly when long delays between attacker actions cause the timeline bounds to degrade.
    \item Introducing Anti-Forensics Confidence ($AF_{conf}$), a Bayesian metric to quantify the likelihood of deliberate log-wiping versus routine evidence loss.
    \item Conducting extensive empirical evaluation and scalability validation across DARPA LLDOS benchmarks~\cite{haines2001extending,darpa2000lldos} and MulVAL enterprise topologies~\cite{mulval} up to 100 hosts, demonstrating robust narrative recovery under severe evidence degradation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Attack Graphs and Alert Correlation}\label{sec:static-ag}

Attack Graphs proactively map how vulnerabilities can be chained across a network~\cite{Noel2018,Lallie2020}. Formally, a static attack graph $G = (E \cup C, R_{r} \cup R_{i})$ is a directed graph mapping exploits and security conditions as edges, as formulated in logic-based models like MulVAL~\cite{mulval,6263942}. Alert correlation techniques match IDS alerts against these prerequisite-consequence relationships to trace attacks~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building,husak2019survey}. However, because $G$ carries no notion of time, it cannot model patches, configuration changes, or causal ordering constraints across windows. Alert correlation systems relying on these \emph{static} graphs can therefore accept alerts on closed paths or construct physically impossible backward-time causal chains, the core limitation motivating F-TAG.

\subsection{Evidence Graphs and Network Forensics}

Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence,he2016network} structure observed digital evidence into graphs for investigative traversal, but stall when logs are missing. Approaches combining them with attack graphs typically use statistical models (Bayesian Networks, HMMs) to estimate missing events or detect falsifications~\cite{liu2012using,zhang2015detecting,Frigault2017}. F-TAG instead treats temporal topology as a hard physical boundary: a falsified timestamp produces an empty bounding intersection, an unambiguous structural contradiction rather than a probability estimate.
  
\subsection{Temporal and Dynamic Network Security}

Dynamic assessment methods recognize that temporal constraints fundamentally change path structures~\cite{Holme,Casteigts2012}, motivating temporal risk variance analysis~\cite{Awan2016}, dynamic attack surface evaluation~\cite{ENOCH2019102448}, and Temporal Attack Graph (TAG) formalisms~\cite{Gain2023}. 

In the proactive TAG model~\cite{Gain2023}, the temporal topology is formalized as a discrete sequence of snapshot attack graphs. These models assess future reachability without incorporating retrospective forensic evidence, the gap F-TAG bridges by converting temporal graph structures into a retrospective forensic engine. \section{Foundational Theory}\label{sec:theory}

To model enterprise network forensics without state-space explosion or causal ambiguity, F-TAG formalizes how time-varying physical topologies constrain reactive digital evidence. This section defines the unified temporal attack graph representation, the constraints governing valid forensic paths, the bounding temporal attack corridor, and the traversal algorithm used to enumerate feasible attack sequences.

\subsection{Definition 1: Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG)}\label{sec:def1}

The F-TAG engine executes a \emph{Reconstruction Phase} to collapse the discrete snapshots of a proactive TAG into a unified mathematical structure where each edge $e$ carries a set of \emph{active windows} $\mathcal{W}(e) \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, \tau-1\}$. This representation captures the physical reality that network edges (vulnerability exploitation paths) appear and disappear over time as configurations change, patches are applied, and firewall rules are modified. The unified representation enables efficient algorithmic traversal without redundant duplication of shared topology across windows.

\textbf{Definition 1 (Forensic Temporal Attack Graph).} Formally, the reconstructed Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG) is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}^a_{unified} = \left(V_{all},\ E_{all},\ \mathcal{W}\right)
\end{equation}
where $V_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} V_t$ is the cumulative set of all vertices, $E_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} E_t$ is the cumulative set of all directed attack edges, and $\mathcal{W}: E_{all} \to 2^{\{0, 1, \dots, \tau-1\}}$ maps each edge to the set of discrete time windows in which it was physically active and traversable (where the exponential notation denotes the power set, indicating an edge may be active in any arbitrary subset of windows).

\paragraph{Physical Walkthrough Example.} 
Consider an investigation spanning four weeks: $w_0, w_1, w_2, w_3$, where the time windows are indexed $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Suppose directed edge $e$ represents the exploitation of a critical web vulnerability (e.g., Log4Shell):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item In Week~0 ($w_0$), the server is unpatched. An attacker can exploit the vulnerability, so Week~0 is active: $0 \in \mathcal{W}(e)$.
    \item In Week~1 ($w_1$), administrators apply a vendor patch, closing the vulnerability: $1 \notin \mathcal{W}(e)$.
    \item In Week~2 ($w_2$), the system remains patched: $2 \notin \mathcal{W}(e)$.
    \item In Week~3 ($w_3$), an IT rollback accidentally restores an unpatched disk image, reopening the flaw: $3 \in \mathcal{W}(e)$.
\end{enumerate}
Under F-TAG, the temporal lifespan for this attack edge is simply $\mathcal{W}(e) = \{0, 3\}$. This informs the forensic investigator of physical ground truth: the attacker could have traversed edge $e$ during Week~0 or Week~3, but traversal during Week~1 or Week~2 was physically impossible. If an IDS alert fires in Week~1 claiming an attacker used this exploit, F-TAG immediately recognizes a structural contradiction. Thus, by attaching a temporal calendar $\mathcal{W}(e)$ to each edge, F-TAG preserves complete historical accuracy without wasting storage on redundant graph copies.

\subsection{Definition 2: Forensic Temporal Attack Path}\label{sec:def2}

\textbf{Definition 2.} Operating over the F-TAG topology, a Forensic Temporal Attack Path $p^f_{ij}$ from node $i$ to node $j$ is a sequence of hops $\langle (e_0, \omega_0), (e_1, \omega_1), \dots, (e_{\eta-1}, \omega_{\eta-1}) \rangle$ satisfying three strict constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Existence Constraint:} Each edge $e_k$ is traversed at an execution window $\omega_k \in \mathcal{W}(e_k)$ (where $\omega$ denotes the specific execution window for the hop, distinct from the absolute time window identifiers $w$), ensuring the exploitation path physically existed at the time it was used.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Constraint:} The execution window sequence is strictly non-decreasing: $\omega_0 \le \omega_1 \le \dots \le \omega_{\eta-1}$, ensuring the attacker cannot travel backward in time.
    \item \textbf{Evidence Constraint:} The span of time windows containing the sequence must not yield an empty intersection when constrained by retrospective observed evidence timestamps.
\end{enumerate}

By evaluating these constraints against the unified $\mathcal{W}$ mapping rather than discrete snapshots, the chronological constraint natively prevents backward-time causal loops while the evidence constraint natively rejects historically impossible narratives. This ensures that every enumerated path represents a physically realizable attack sequence consistent with forensic observation.

\subsection{Definition 3: Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC)}\label{sec:tac}

\textbf{Definition 3 (Temporal Attack Corridor).} For a Forensic Temporal Attack Path $p^f_{ij}$ comprising $\eta$ hops, the Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC) is the bounding span of discrete time windows containing the entire attack sequence, from initial breach to final objective:
\begin{equation}
\text{TAC}(p^f_{ij}) = [\omega_0(p^f_{ij}),\ \omega_{\eta-1}(p^f_{ij})]
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0(p^f_{ij})$ is the time window in which the initial hop was executed (e.g., initial compromise at entry node $i$), and $\omega_{\eta-1}(p^f_{ij})$ is the time window of the final hop (e.g., reaching the target objective at node $j$).

For example, if an attacker executes the first hop in Week~0 ($\omega_0 = 0$) and concludes the final hop in Week~2 ($\omega_{\eta-1} = 2$), the corridor is the window span $[w_0, w_2]$ (covering windows $\{0, 1, 2\}$).

The TAC encapsulates the physical lifespan of a specific attack narrative, structurally enforcing the earliest possible start window and latest possible completion window permitted by the evolving network topology. Any retrospective evidence that falls outside this window span signals an unambiguous structural contradiction, natively identifying either an impossible false-positive alert or deliberate timestamp tampering.

\subsection{Path Enumeration Algorithm}\label{sec:enumeration}

Given a suspect entry host $i$ (such as an external web server) and a target asset $j$ (such as an internal database), a forensic investigator must discover all structurally feasible attack paths between them. Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate} solves this by executing a time-constrained depth-first search over the unified graph $\mathcal{G}^a_{unified}$.

\paragraph{Computational Intractability of Static Filtering.}
As established in Section 1, simply generating all paths on a static graph and filtering out impossible ``Frankenstein paths'' after the fact is computationally intractable. In enterprise networks, the number of static paths grows combinatorially, quickly overwhelming system memory. By enforcing the temporal condition $\omega \ge \omega_{min}$ directly during traversal, Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate} cuts off invalid branches at the root, ensuring the search scales efficiently.

\paragraph{Guaranteed Completeness.}
Because this temporal condition discards only those transitions where $\omega < \omega_{min}$, it eliminates exclusively those sequences that violate the physical arrow of time. No chronologically feasible attack path is ever skipped. Consequently, the search is mathematically guaranteed to recover every valid attack path of length $\le H_{max}$ between node $i$ and node $j$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Temporal Path Enumeration ($Q^a_{ij}$)}
\label{alg:enumerate}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE F-TAG $\mathcal{G}^a$, source node $i$, target node $j$, max hops $H_{max}$
\ENSURE Set of all valid temporal attack paths $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \emptyset$
\STATE $\text{Stack} \gets \{(i, 0, \langle\rangle)\}$ \COMMENT{(node, min\_window, path)}
\WHILE{Stack $\neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $(n_{curr}, \omega_{min}, \text{path}) \gets \text{Stack.pop()}$
    \IF{$n_{curr} = j$ \AND $|\text{path}| > 0$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \mathcal{P}_{ij} \cup \{\text{TemporalPath(path)}\}$
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$|\text{path}| \ge H_{max}$}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \FORALL{edges $e$ from $n_{curr}$}
        \FORALL{$\omega \in \mathcal{W}(e)$ where $\omega \ge \omega_{min}$}
            \STATE Stack.push$(e.dst, \omega, \text{path} \cup \{(e, \omega)\})$
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{The F-TAG Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

The F-TAG methodology bridges physical network evolution with reactive digital evidence (such as IDS alerts, firewall logs, and authentication records) to reconstruct attack narratives when evidence is incomplete or contradictory.

\subsection{Path-Level Forensic Bounds}\label{sec:path-metrics}

When reconstructing an incident, relying solely on raw log timestamps artificially narrows the perceived timeline: missing or wiped logs at the edges of an attack make the intrusion appear to have started later and ended earlier than it truly did. The \textbf{Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC)} bounds the true incident timeline by combining surviving forensic evidence with the physical constraints of the network topology.

Given an attack path $p$ consisting of $H$ hops and a set of $N$ observed evidence timestamps $\mathcal{E} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N\}$ mapped to discrete time windows. Intuitively, FWoC asks: given whatever evidence survived and how the network was actually configured, what is the tightest window in which the intrusion could have started and ended? It is the overlap between what the surviving evidence directly supports and what the Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC) physically permits:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwoc}
\text{FWoC}(p) = \text{TAC}(p) \;\cap\; \left[\min(\mathcal{E}) - \delta - \delta_s,\;\;\max(\mathcal{E}) + \delta + \delta_s\right]
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:fwoc} balances two complementary components: an \emph{evidence-bounded interval} derived from surviving telemetry, and a \emph{structural corridor} enforced by network topology.

The raw evidence span $[\min(\mathcal{E}), \max(\mathcal{E})]$ captures the earliest and latest timestamps recorded in surviving logs or alerts. To absorb network transit latency, NTP drift, and timestamp discretization errors across diverse logging hosts, the tolerance parameter $\delta$ (typically 0 or 1 window) provides a small clock-skew margin.

To mathematically guarantee that the true intrusion boundaries are captured even when evidence is missing, the topology-aware correction term $\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)$ expands the raw evidence bounds outward.\label{sec:worst-case-bounds} This accounts for the worst-case scenario where up to $H - N$ unobserved contiguous actions occurred entirely before the first recorded alert or entirely after the last one. When evidence is complete ($N \ge H$), $\delta_s = 0$ and the bound tightens purely around the observed timestamps.

Finally, the intersection ($\cap$) with the Temporal Attack Corridor $\text{TAC}(p) = [\omega_0(p), \omega_{\eta-1}(p)]$ acts as a physical ceiling and floor. Physical network topology imposes hard constraints: an attacker cannot exploit a vulnerability before it was introduced, nor after it was patched. The TAC clamp thus confines the evidence interval strictly to windows where the path was physically traversable, preventing arbitrary outward expansion. For example, if an attack path has $H=5$ total hops but evidence survives for only $N=2$ of those hops, the correction term $\delta_s=3$ expands the evidence bounds outward by 3 time windows, while the TAC clamp securely confines it to the physically feasible corridor.

\paragraph{Structural Contradiction Detection.}
If the intersection in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fwoc} yields an empty set:
\begin{equation}
\text{FWoC}(p) = \emptyset
\end{equation}
the evidence timestamps are physically incompatible with the network topology. For example, if an IDS alert fires in Week~1 for an exploit path that only existed in Week~3, the evidence and structural corridor do not overlap. This signals an unambiguous \emph{structural contradiction}, proving with mathematical certainty that the alert is a false positive, the log timestamp was falsified, or the hypothesized attack path is incorrect. Static attack graphs cannot detect such contradictions because they lack a temporal frame of reference.

\subsection{Topological Triage \& Scoping}\label{sec:network-metrics}

While path-level metrics evaluate individual candidate routes, incident response teams require network-wide prioritization to identify which hosts must be isolated and imaged first, and how far back into historical log archives an investigation must reach. To support operational scoping without manual inspection of hundreds of hosts, F-TAG aggregates surviving attack paths across the entire topology to compute three structural metrics.

To pinpoint mandatory forensic chokepoints, hosts that the adversary was forced to traverse regardless of the specific route executed, making them the highest-priority targets for memory acquisition, the \emph{Temporal Chokepoint Score} $\chi(h)$ measures the fraction of evidentially surviving attack paths traversing each host $h$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chokepoint}
\chi(h) = \frac{|\{p \in \mathcal{P}_{surviving} : h \in V(p)\}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}|}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_{surviving}$ denotes the set of candidate attack paths that remain valid after applying temporal and evidence constraints, and $V(p)$ is the set of hosts visited along path $p$. A score of $\chi(h) = 1.0$ confirms that host $h$ lies on every structurally plausible narrative.



Finally, to provide an immutable structural anchor for how deep into cold storage investigators must search, proving that any external activity prior to this point is physically disconnected from the observed compromise, even under total perimeter log deletion, the \emph{Temporal Search Radius} $T_{min}(j_{tgt})$ bounds the earliest possible breach window:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tmin}
T_{min}(j_{tgt}) = \min_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{\to j_{tgt}}} \omega_0(p)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}_{\to j_{tgt}}$ is the set of topologically feasible backward-reachable attack paths terminating at a downstream victim asset $j_{tgt}$ (such as a database exfiltration event), and $\omega_0(p)$ is the initial entry execution window of path $p$.

\subsection{Anti-Forensics Confidence (\texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf})}\label{sec:afconf}

When an attack path contains an unobserved hop, investigators must distinguish routine logging failure (log rotation, network packet drops) from deliberate anti-forensic wiping. The Anti-Forensics Confidence metric ($AF_{conf}$) provides a secondary, Bayesian assessment that estimates the likelihood of deliberate tampering without triggering misleading false accusations.

\paragraph{The Bayesian Formulation.}
To help investigators formally distinguish whether missing evidence is due to routine logging failure or a deliberate anti-forensic cover-up, the posterior probability of deliberate log-wiping given an evidence gap $G$ at node $n$ with acquired privilege level $x$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:afconf}
AF_{conf}(G) = P(H_1 \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $H_1$ is the hypothesis that the evidence was \textbf{deliberately wiped} by an attacker.
    \item $H_0$ is the hypothesis that the evidence was lost through \textbf{benign causes} (routine maintenance, logging failure).
    \item $P(H_1)$ and $P(H_0) = 1 - P(H_1)$ are prior probabilities reflecting baseline operational suspicion.
    \item $x \in \{\text{root}, \text{user}\}$ is the privilege state the attacker acquired on node $n$ (where ``root'' indicates full administrative access, and ``user'' indicates restricted, standard access).
\end{itemize}

The likelihood terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:afconf} are grounded directly in operating system access-control principles. In modern operating systems, unprivileged users cannot modify or erase security event logs (such as \texttt{/var/log/audit/}, Windows Security Event Logs, or endpoint detection telemetry), tampering with audit records strictly requires administrative or root privileges. Consequently, under the deliberate wiping hypothesis ($H_1$), the adversary is predominantly expected to have acquired root access:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{root} \mid H_1) = 0.75, \quad P(\text{user} \mid H_1) = 0.25
\end{equation}
Grounding likelihood in operating system permissions provides robust discrimination that avoids reliance on brittle anti-forensic tool signatures, which sophisticated adversaries routinely evade.

Conversely, benign logging failures (e.g., buffer overflows or network packet drops) happen indiscriminately across infrastructure regardless of host privilege. Under $H_0$, the probability of observing a missing record at a root-privileged node simply equals the empirical proportion of root nodes across the target network topology:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:base-rate}
P(\text{root} \mid H_0) = P_{root,H_0} = \frac{|\{n \in V_{all} : \text{priv}(n) = \text{root}\}|}{|V_{all}|}
\end{equation}
Because F-TAG computes $P_{root,H_0}$ directly from the network topology under investigation, $AF_{conf}$ automatically calibrates itself to any environment (such as high-privilege server clusters versus locked-down workstation subnets).

Two analytical properties characterize the mathematical behavior of $AF_{conf}$:

\begin{lemma}[Monotonicity under Uniform Base Rates]
When structural base rates are uniform across privilege levels, $AF_{conf}(G)$ is strictly non-decreasing with respect to the attacker's privilege level at the point of evidence loss.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Let $x_{root}$ and $x_{user}$ represent root and user-level privileges. By our domain grounding, $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$. Holding the structural base rate equal ($P(x_{root} \mid H_0) = P(x_{user} \mid H_0) = c$), the posterior ratio $\frac{AF_{conf}(G_{root})}{AF_{conf}(G_{user})}$ simplifies via the monotonically increasing function $f(y) = \frac{a y}{a y + b}$. Since $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$, it follows that $AF_{conf}(G_{root}) > AF_{conf}(G_{user})$. Intuitively, higher privilege levels always increase the posterior when base rates are held equal (e.g., under a 10\% prior and 20\% root-node base rate, this raises the posterior to roughly 29\% at a root node versus 3\% at a user node). $\square$

\textit{Boundary Condition (Root Saturation Inversion):} In networks saturated with root-privileged nodes ($p \to 1$), the base rate for non-root nodes approaches zero ($P(x_{user} \mid H_0) \to 0$). Here, missing evidence at a rare non-root node produces an artificially high $AF_{conf}$ score, inverting the expected ordering.

\begin{lemma}[Root-Node Posterior Limit under Saturation]
As the network becomes saturated with root-level nodes ($P(x \mid H_0) \to 1$), the $AF_{conf}$ score for root nodes drops strictly below the prior $P(H_1)$.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Let $P(x \mid H_0) \to 1$. With operational setting $P(x \mid H_1) = \alpha = 0.75$, the limit is $\frac{0.75 P(H_1)}{0.75 P(H_1) + P(H_0)}$. Because $P(H_1) < 1$ and $P(H_0) > 0$, this is strictly less than the prior $P(H_1)$. The limit only approaches $P(H_1)$ if we assume deliberate wiping \textit{always} occurs at root nodes ($\alpha \to 1$).

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

To evaluate F-TAG, we assess its performance across two complementary environments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Historically Verified Intrusions (DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2)}~\cite{haines2001extending,darpa2000lldos}: Provides packet-level ground truth to test timeline reconstruction, FWoC bounds under evidence degradation, topological triage stability, and adversarial falsification detection.
    \item \textbf{Scalable Enterprise Topologies (MulVAL)}~\cite{mulval}: Generates multi-tier enterprise networks to validate temporal Datalog mapping, Bayesian $AF_{conf}$ anti-forensics calibration, and computational scalability up to 100 hosts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Environments and Setup}\label{sec:eval-setup}

\paragraph{DARPA LLDOS Benchmarks.} 
We mapped the verified capability paths from both LLDOS scenarios into F-TAGs, using the real, historical packet captures and IDS alerts from the dataset as our evidence base. LLDOS 1.0 represents an attacker executing a multi-stage distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack across multiple hosts (\texttt{mill}, \texttt{pascal}, \texttt{locke}), exhibiting topological path redundancy. In contrast, LLDOS 2.0.2 models a stealthy, serial intrusion executing without topological redundancy.

\paragraph{Enterprise Topology Generation.} 
We used MulVAL~\cite{mulval} to generate synthetic multi-tier enterprise network topologies and attack graphs across discrete time windows, modeling time-varying vulnerability lifespans and configuration changes. Because these network structures are synthetic, log evidence across these paths was simulated mathematically via a Monte Carlo injection pipeline to rigorously test our metrics at scale.

\paragraph{Static vs. Temporal Attack Feasibility.} 
To highlight how temporal structure constrains forensic analysis, we evaluated a 5-hop attack path (across 6 hosts) over an observation timeline of $\tau=20$ discrete time windows, where the required vulnerabilities coexisted only during the window span $[w_8, w_{13}]$. While a static graph collapses this history into a single timeless path, blind to when vulnerabilities actually existed, F-TAG identifies the 252 valid temporal execution paths (all non-decreasing hop sequences satisfying $\omega_0 \le \dots \le \omega_4$ within $[w_8, w_{13}]$, Fig.~\ref{fig:static-vs-tag}). Crucially, an alert observed outside this active window (such as a false alarm at $w_0$) produces an empty intersection ($\text{FWoC} = \emptyset$), allowing F-TAG to natively filter impossible false positives that static attack graphs accept as valid.

\paragraph{Static Graphs Miss Both Falsifications, F-TAG Catches Both.}
A static graph has no concept of time, so it scores 0.00 recall on both State Revocation and Window Closure (Table~\ref{tab:falsification}, Fig.~\ref{fig:falsification}), it simply cannot tell a closed path from an open one. F-TAG detects both via structural contradiction ($\text{FWoC} = \emptyset$), reaching perfect recall under a full infrastructure wipe.

The one tradeoff is precision, and it tracks network redundancy directly: LLDOS 1.0 has parallel attack routes, so benign timestamp inaccuracies (e.g., clock skew or network logging delays) on one path are absorbed by the others (0.88 to 0.93 precision). LLDOS 2.0.2 is a single serial path with no redundancy, so these same inaccuracies have nowhere to go and precision drops to 0.70, roughly 3 false contradiction flags per 10. \subsection{Bayesian Anti-Forensics Calibration (\texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf})}\label{sec:results-afconf}

The central operational question is whether $AF_{conf}$ can reliably tell deliberate log-wiping apart from routine telemetry loss, and whether that discrimination depends on how suspicious we start out (the prior $P(H_1)$).

To evaluate this, we calibrated $AF_{conf}$ using a Monte Carlo injection pipeline across 10 random seeds and three prior settings ($P(H_1) \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$) on the MulVAL enterprise topology (15 candidate paths, $\sim$45 samples per class per seed), testing three distinct evidence-loss classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wipe-Class ($H_1$)}: Deliberate log-wiping biased toward high-privilege pivot nodes ($10\times$ weight for root status) and scaled by temporal chokepoint score $\chi$.
    \item \textbf{Random-Benign ($H_0$)}: Uniformly distributed evidence loss across all path nodes, modeling accidental failure.
    \item \textbf{Rotation-Benign ($H_0$)}: Periodic log loss recurring at fixed machines, modeling scheduled log rotation.
\end{enumerate}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Figures/fig_fwoc_afconf.png}
\caption{(a) FWoC soundness and tightness under evidence loss on LLDOS. (b) Discriminative power (AUC) of $AF_{conf}$ across prior settings $P(H_1)$ on MulVAL.}
\label{fig:fwoc-afconf}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{It Does Not Depend on the Prior.}
Lemma~\ref{lemma:prior-invariance} proves that the ranking $AF_{conf}$ induces is strictly prior-invariant, Fig.~\ref{fig:fwoc-afconf}b confirms this empirically, with the AUC remaining at 0.801 (Wipe vs. Random loss) across every prior tested.

\begin{lemma}[Prior-Invariance of Ranking]\label{lemma:prior-invariance}
For fixed $x \in \{\text{root}, \text{non-root}\}$, the posterior $AF_{conf}(x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)P(H_0)}$ is a strictly increasing function of the likelihood ratio $LR(x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)}{P(x \mid H_0)}$ for any $P(H_1) \in (0,1)$. Since the set of achievable $LR$ values (and hence the induced ranking over samples) is independent of $P(H_1)$, the ROC curve, and therefore AUC, is invariant to the choice of prior.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Write $AF_{conf}(x) = \frac{LR(x)P(H_1)}{LR(x)P(H_1) + (1-P(H_1))}$, which is strictly increasing in $LR(x)$ for any fixed $P(H_1) \in (0,1)$ since $\frac{\partial AF_{conf}}{\partial LR} = \frac{P(H_1)(1-P(H_1))}{(\cdot)^2} > 0$. As $LR(x)$ does not depend on $P(H_1)$, the ordering of $AF_{conf}$ across the two values of $x$ is preserved for every $P(H_1)$, so the ROC curve (which depends only on this ordering) is unchanged. $\square$

\paragraph{It Discriminates Well, but Not Perfectly.}
An AUC of $\approx 0.80$ means $AF_{conf}$ reliably separates deliberate wiping from accidental loss, but with real overlap, which is expected, since our only signal is a binary root/non-root privilege flag. Rotation-triggered loss is slightly noisier ($\text{AUC} = 0.800$, wider 95\% CI: [0.742, 0.858]) because scheduled rotation only ever touches a fixed subset of hosts.

\paragraph{A Threshold Cutoff Eliminates False Accusations.}
Treating $AF_{conf} \ge 0.85$ as the accusation cutoff drives the false-accusation rate (FAR) to 0.0\% against both benign-loss classes (Table~\ref{tab:far}), at the cost of only flagging the clearest cases. Below 0.70, FAR sits around 25\%, too high for an accusation, which is why we recommend the higher threshold operationally.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Operational Context}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}'s constraint eliminates static graphs' three structural failure modes (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). Where Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence} build narratives bottom-up and stall without logs, F-TAG operates top-down, injecting evidence as strict filters over a fixed topology, providing resilience via FWoC bounds, native contradiction detection, and a $T_{min}$ anchor regardless of evidence loss. Path enumeration is bounded by $O(k_{max}^{H_{max}})$, but early termination keeps effective out-degree small, metric computation takes at most $O(|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}| \cdot H_{max})$ (Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability}). In practice, TAG generation runs proactively during vulnerability management, and the reactive engine fuses SIEM alerts on demand to compute FWoC, prioritize hosts, and flag tampering.

\subsection{Operational Considerations}\label{sec:threats}

While F-TAG provides rigorous structural bounds on forensic data, its practical deployment involves specific operational considerations. First, the Bayesian $AF_{conf}$ metric was calibrated using a foundational binary privilege model (root vs. non-root). While this provides robust baseline discrimination, future integration with granular access control frameworks (e.g., SELinux or Active Directory hierarchies) would further refine its precision on live SIEM telemetry. Second, while F-TAG's computational cost scales predictably within typical enterprise topologies, highly dense architectures may benefit from standard hierarchical abstraction (such as subnet aggregation) to maintain real-time triage speeds. Finally, the methodology assumes that the underlying network topology configuration is collected out-of-band and serves as a tamper-resistant ground truth. Ensuring that topology generation and vulnerability scanning are isolated from potentially compromised infrastructure is a standard prerequisite for reliable forensic analysis.

\paragraph{Adversarial Dormancy Boundary.}\label{sec:adversarial-dormancy} The worst-case $\delta_s$ correction (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}) mathematically guarantees containment under a contiguous-execution assumption. To understand the operational limits of this bound, we stress-tested it against a simulated dormant Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) exhibiting an extreme 18-window gap between reconnaissance and exploitation (Table~\ref{tab:dormant-apt}). As expected under severe dormancy with sparse evidence ($N=1$), the standard bound prioritizes tightness (17.9\% width) over absolute containment. As evidence completeness increases, soundness reliably recovers to 100\%. This demonstrates that while the bounds are highly robust for typical intrusions, analysts investigating suspected long-dormancy campaigns can simply widen the $\delta_s$ tolerance parameter to safely absorb extended inter-hop delays.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This work addressed the core question of whether a temporally evolving attack topology can serve as a structural constraint for forensic reconstruction. By formalizing F-TAG, it was demonstrated that fusing reactive evidence with proactive topologies provides robust narrative recovery. The Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) was derived to establish conditional soundness guarantees on unobserved actions (under a contiguous-execution assumption), proving that strict temporal enforcement naturally filters causality bugs and falsified timestamps. The limits of these bounds were mathematically characterized under long-dormancy adversarial behavior, and the approach was validated extensively across LLDOS benchmarks and scaled enterprise topologies. (Secondary explorations into Bayesian log-wiping probabilities achieved a 0.0\% false-accusation rate, pending real-world validation). 
\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future-work}

Future work will focus on multi-level privilege modeling (e.g., SELinux) to improve $AF_{conf}$ discriminative power. Additionally, while the worst-case bound correctness was validated here under $H \approx \tau$, future experiments will empirically demonstrate FWoC narrowing under the $H \ll \tau$ regime (short attack paths embedded in long capture windows). Operational deployment necessitates cross-topology testing and calibration against live SIEM data with verified anti-forensic ground truth. Finally, integrating F-TAG with live SIEM pipelines would allow the FWoC to dynamically narrow during active incident triage.
