% ABSTRACT
% ================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Modern network forensics heavily relies on the reactive reconstruction of digital evidence, such as intrusion detection alerts and system logs. However, digital evidence is fundamentally fragile, as it is prone to accidental loss and vulnerable to deliberate anti-forensic tampering. Traditional Evidence Graphs attempt to reconstruct attack paths purely from observed logs, breaking down completely when evidence is missing. Furthermore, attempting to correlate alerts over legacy static attack graphs ignores the physical reality of evolving network topologies, leading to causality bugs and false positives. In this paper, we introduce the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG), a triage engine that fuses proactive, time-aware structural risk models with reactive forensic evidence. By injecting digital evidence as strict constraints upon the physical topology, F-TAG naturally detects and filters temporal contradictions. As its primary result, when evidence is missing or wiped, F-TAG computes a Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC), which provides a provable bound on unobserved attacker actions under F-TAG's topological assumptions. Secondarily, we explore the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric ($AF_{conf}$), an exploratory Bayesian posterior that leverages granular privilege topologies to probabilistically flag deliberate log tampering. We demonstrate the efficacy of F-TAG on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated enterprise-style topologies up to 100 hosts, successfully recovering complete attack narratives despite severe evidence degradation.

\textbf{Keywords:} Network Forensics, Temporal Attack Graphs, Anti-Forensics, Bayesian Analysis
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Imagine an investigator called in after a suspected breach. The only visible trace is a single IDS alert on the internal database server. Every log on the upstream web server is gone. Did the attacker come through a known vulnerability that was patched last week? Or through a different path entirely? Was the log wiped deliberately? Without answers to these questions, the investigation stalls~\cite{khan2022digital}. This scenario is not a corner case: modern attackers operating as Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) routinely wipe logs, falsify timestamps, and exploit brief vulnerability windows to cover their tracks~\cite{Niu2016,milajerdi2019holmes,zhang2015detecting}.

Two families of tools exist to help investigators, but each is blind to what the other sees. \emph{Evidence Graphs}~\cite{wang2008evidence} reconstruct causal chains from observed logs and alerts. They are powerful when evidence is complete, but when a log is wiped, the chain simply breaks. The investigation stalls with disconnected fragments. \emph{Static Attack Graphs}~\cite{1004377,Lallie2020} take the opposite approach: they map the full set of vulnerability exploitation paths across the network. Researchers have tried correlating IDS alerts against these graphs to fill evidence gaps~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building}. However, a static attack graph collapses the entire network history into one timeless snapshot, and this is a critical flaw for forensics.

\textbf{Why a static attack graph is not enough.} Consider a vulnerability patched on Tuesday. In the real world, the exploitation path ceased to exist the moment the patch was applied. But a static attack graph, which has no concept of time, still shows that path as open. If an IDS alert fires on Wednesday, a static-graph-based system will happily accept that the attacker exploited the (already patched) vulnerability on Wednesday, producing a false and misleading attack reconstruction. Simply attaching timestamps to static graph edges is insufficient because the problem is \emph{path-stitching}. A path through a network consists of multiple hops, and the chronological ordering constraint must be enforced \emph{across the entire hop sequence}. Without this structural guarantee, a standard graph traversal algorithm can stitch together an early edge (say, from Week~1) with a late edge (from Week~3) via an intermediate node that was patched in Week~2, producing an attack path that was never physically possible. Fusing chronological evidence with a timeless graph produces three types of concrete failures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dormant Attacker Illusion:} A topology change blocks the attacker at hop $k$. The static graph, unaware of the change, misdiagnoses the gap as the attacker intentionally lying dormant, rather than as a detection opportunity.
    \item \textbf{False-Positive Propagation:} An IDS alert fires on an edge that was patched before the alert timestamp. The static system accepts it; a temporal check would instantly flag it as a contradiction.
    \item \textbf{Combinatorial Intersection Bugs:} Multiple alerts at different times are stored on the same static node. The traversal algorithm then constructs backward-time causal chains where a later exploitation is treated as the prerequisite for an earlier one, which is physically impossible.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome both the fragility of reactive evidence and the temporal blindness of static graphs, we introduce the \textbf{Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG)}. The key insight is architectural: rather than building a graph from uncertain forensic evidence, F-TAG starts from a proactive, time-aware model of the network's evolving physical topology (the Temporal Attack Graph~\cite{Gain2023}) and injects forensic evidence as strict \emph{constraints} upon it. The topology provides a fixed, tamper-resistant reference frame. Evidence either fits within that frame, or it is flagged as a contradiction. When evidence is missing, the topology bounds what the attacker could have done during the gap.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formalize the F-TAG framework, which enforces chronological sequencing on observed evidence and naturally filters false-positive noise and causality bugs through topological contradiction detection.
    \item We introduce the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC), which computes provable bounds on unobserved attacker actions when evidence is missing.
    \item We present the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric ($AF_{conf}$), a Bayesian posterior that quantifies the likelihood of deliberate log-wiping versus benign loss by exploiting topological privilege distributions. This is a secondary, exploratory metric.
    \item We validate F-TAG extensively on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated topologies up to 100 hosts, demonstrating robustness under evidence degradation, calibrating anti-forensic sensitivity, and confirming that F-TAG detects temporally-dependent falsification scenarios where static methods fail.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents the foundational theory. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the F-TAG framework and its metrics. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} details the experimental setup and presents the results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes, and Section~\ref{sec:future-work} outlines future directions.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Attack Graphs and Alert Correlation}

Attack Graphs proactively map how vulnerabilities can be chained across a network~\cite{Noel2018,Lallie2020}. Alert correlation techniques match IDS alerts against these prerequisite-consequence relationships to trace attacks and hypothesize missing alerts~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building,husak2019survey}. However, these methods rely on \emph{static} attack graphs, which lack temporal ordering. Matching timestamped alerts against static graphs can result in accepting alerts on closed paths or constructing physically impossible backward-time causal chains.

\subsection{Evidence Graphs and Network Forensics}

Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence,he2016network} structure observed digital evidence into graphs for investigative traversal, but stall when logs are missing. To address this, several approaches combine them with attack graphs using statistical models (e.g., Bayesian Networks, HMMs) to estimate missing events or detect falsifications~\cite{liu2012using,zhang2015detecting,Frigault2017}. Rather than estimating statistically, F-TAG treats temporal topology as a hard physical boundary: a falsified timestamp produces an empty bounding intersection, signaling an unambiguous structural contradiction.


\subsection{Temporal and Dynamic Network Security}

Dynamic assessment methods recognize that temporal constraints fundamentally change path structures~\cite{Holme,Casteigts2012}. This has led to approaches for temporal risk variance~\cite{Awan2016}, dynamic attack surface evaluation~\cite{ENOCH2019102448}, and Temporal Attack Graph (TAG) formalisms~\cite{Gain2023}. However, all these models are forward-looking: they assess future risk without incorporating retrospective forensic evidence. F-TAG bridges this gap by converting the proactive TAG framework into a retrospective forensic engine.

\subsection{Research Gap}

The literature reviewed above reveals a clear divide between two families of approaches that have developed independently:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reactive forensic methods} (Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence}, alert correlation~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,1366134}) consume digital evidence to reconstruct what happened, but they depend entirely on the evidence being present. When logs are wiped or timestamps are falsified, these methods produce incomplete or misleading results because their graph structure is built from the evidence itself.
    \item \textbf{Proactive temporal models} (TI-HARM~\cite{ENOCH2019102448}, TAG~\cite{Gain2023}) capture how the network's physical topology evolves over time, but they are designed for forward-looking risk assessment. They have no mechanism to incorporate observed forensic evidence from a past incident.
\end{itemize}

No existing framework combines both capabilities. F-TAG bridges this divide with a simple but powerful architectural choice: it treats the proactive temporal topology as a fixed reference frame and injects the reactive forensic evidence as strict filtering constraints upon it.

Given only the $W_2$ alert, an Evidence Graph stalls with a hole where the Web Server log should be. A static attack graph, oblivious to the patch, reconstructs the Internet$\to$Web Server (via CVE)$\to$Database path and accepts it as valid at $W_2$ --- a physically impossible timeline that sends the investigator down a false lead. F-TAG, knowing the CVE edge existed only in $W_0$, immediately rejects the $W_2$ path as a structural contradiction and instead computes a Forensic Window of Compromise $[W_0, W_1]$, bounding the true breach despite the missing log. Probabilistic alternatives such as Hidden Markov Models~\cite{Frigault2008} or Bayesian Networks~\cite{Frigault2017} can estimate missing events but cannot provide structural guarantees: when an attacker wipes an entire log block, statistical models lack training data to distinguish deliberate wiping from a system failure, whereas F-TAG's topological constraint produces a formal, empty-intersection contradiction signal.

\section{Foundational Theory}\label{sec:theory}

The foundation of F-TAG relies on rigorous complex network theory to avoid state-space explosion and causal ambiguity. We formalize the core definitions that underpin the entire framework, building upon the Temporal Attack Graph formalism introduced by Gain and Barik~\cite{Gain2023}.

\subsection{Static Attack Graph Formulation}\label{sec:static-ag}

A static attack graph $G = (E \cup C, R_{r} \cup R_{i})$ is a directed graph mapping exploits and security conditions as edges~\cite{6263942}. Unlike the temporal formulation introduced next, $G$ carries no notion of time and therefore cannot model patches, configuration changes, or causal ordering constraints across windows --- the core limitation motivating F-TAG.

\subsection{Definition 1: Temporal Attack Graph}\label{sec:def1}

\textbf{Definition 1.} A Temporal Attack Graph $\mathcal{G}^a(t_{min}, t_{max})$ is defined as a sequence of discrete attack graph snapshots $(G_0, G_1, \dots, G_{\tau-1})$, where each snapshot $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$ represents the network's vulnerability topology at time window $t$~\cite{Gain2023}. The node set $V_t$ contains host-state pairs representing machines and their security-relevant states (e.g., \texttt{mill:root\_access}), while the edge set $E_t$ contains exploitation paths that are physically possible at time $t$.

The F-TAG engine executes a \emph{Reconstruction Phase} to collapse these discrete snapshots into a unified mathematical structure where each edge $e$ carries a set of \emph{active windows} $\mathcal{W}(e) \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, \tau-1\}$. This representation captures the physical reality that network edges (vulnerability exploitation paths) appear and disappear over time as configurations change, patches are applied, and firewall rules are modified. The unified representation enables efficient algorithmic traversal without redundant duplication of shared topology across windows.

Formally, the reconstructed F-TAG is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}^a_{unified} = \left(V_{all},\ E_{all},\ \mathcal{W}\right)
\end{equation}
where $V_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} V_t$, $E_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} E_t$, and $\mathcal{W}: E_{all} \to 2^{\{0,\dots,\tau-1\}}$ maps each edge to the set of time windows in which it physically exists.

\subsection{Definition 2: Temporal Attack Path}\label{sec:def2}

\textbf{Definition 2.} A temporal attack path $p^a_{ij}$ from node $i$ to node $j$ is a sequence of hops $\langle (e_0, w_0), (e_1, w_1), \dots, (e_{\eta-1}, w_{\eta-1}) \rangle$ satisfying two constraints~\cite{Gain2023}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Existence Constraint:} Each edge $e_k$ is traversed at window $w_k \in \mathcal{W}(e_k)$, ensuring the exploitation path must physically exist at the time it is used.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Constraint:} The window sequence is strictly non-decreasing: $w_0 \le w_1 \le \dots \le w_{\eta-1}$, ensuring the attacker cannot travel backward in time.
\end{enumerate}

The non-decreasing constraint natively prevents impossible backward-time causal loops, ensuring that every enumerated path represents a physically realizable attack sequence. This is a fundamental advantage over static graphs, where pathfinding algorithms are oblivious to time.

\subsection{Definition 3: Temporal Attack Corridor}\label{sec:tac}

\textbf{Definition 3.} For a temporal attack path $p$, the Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC) is defined as the bounding interval of the window sequence:
\begin{equation}
\text{TAC}(p) = [w_0(p),\ w_{\eta-1}(p)]
\end{equation}


\subsection{Path Enumeration Algorithm}\label{sec:enumeration}

The temporal path enumeration follows a depth-first search (Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}). The chronological pruning at Line 14 restricts traversal to $w \ge w_{min}$, preventing the generation of impossible "Frankenstein" paths stitched across non-overlapping vulnerability windows, a critical failure mode of naive static intersection.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Temporal Path Enumeration ($Q^a_{ij}$)}
\label{alg:enumerate}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE F-TAG $\mathcal{G}^a$, source node $i$, target node $j$, max hops $H_{max}$
\ENSURE Set of all valid temporal attack paths $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \emptyset$
\STATE $\text{Stack} \gets \{(i, 0, \langle\rangle)\}$ \COMMENT{(node, min\_window, path)}
\WHILE{Stack $\neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $(n_{curr}, w_{min}, \text{path}) \gets \text{Stack.pop()}$
    \IF{$n_{curr} = j$ \AND $|\text{path}| > 0$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \mathcal{P}_{ij} \cup \{\text{TemporalPath(path)}\}$
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$|\text{path}| \ge H_{max}$}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \FORALL{edges $e$ from $n_{curr}$}
        \FORALL{$w \in \mathcal{W}(e)$ where $w \ge w_{min}$}
            \STATE Stack.push$(e.dst, w, \text{path} \cup \{(e, w)\})$
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Completeness of Path Enumeration.}
We formally prove that Algorithm 1 is complete, i.e., it discovers all valid temporal attack paths without missing any structurally feasible corridors.

\textit{Theorem 1 (Completeness of Temporal Enumeration):} Let $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$ be the set of all paths from $i$ to $j$ returned by Algorithm 1. If there exists a temporal path $p^*$ from $i$ to $j$ satisfying the non-decreasing chronological constraints of Definition 2 with length $|p^*| \le H_{max}$, then $p^* \in \mathcal{P}_{ij}$.

\textit{Proof (sketch):}
Suppose a valid path $p^* = ((e_1,w_1),\dots,(e_n,w_n))$ with $n \le H_{max}$ is not returned. Since the DFS explores all outgoing edges up to depth $H_{max}$ (with revisits permitted), the only mechanism for missing $p^*$ is if the chronological filter at Line~12 prunes some edge $(e_k,w_k)$, meaning the algorithm holds $w_{min} > w_k$ at that step. But the algorithm sets $w_{min} = w_{k-1}$ on arrival at $e_k.\text{src}$, so $w_{min} > w_k$ implies $w_{k-1} > w_k$, contradicting the non-decreasing constraint that $p^*$ must satisfy by Definition~2. Therefore no valid edge is ever pruned, and $p^*$ must be discovered. $\square$

\section{The F-TAG Framework}\label{sec:methodology}

Section~\ref{sec:theory} formalized the proactive temporal graph theory that F-TAG inherits from prior TAG research. This section introduces the forensic engine itself: how F-TAG consumes observed evidence (IDS alerts, firewall logs, system timestamps) and produces actionable investigative outputs.

The framework is organized around three layers. First, \emph{path-level forensic bounds} (Section~\ref{sec:path-metrics}) answer questions about individual attack paths: how wide is the window of compromise, how well does the evidence fit, and how fast did the attacker move? Second, \emph{topological triage} (Section~\ref{sec:network-metrics}) aggregates those path-level results into a network-wide view, identifying which hosts the investigator should examine first and how far back in time to search. Third, the \emph{Anti-Forensics Confidence} metric (Section~\ref{sec:afconf}) adds a probabilistic layer that flags evidence gaps that are likely caused by deliberate log-wiping rather than routine loss.

\subsection{Path-Level Forensic Bounds}\label{sec:path-metrics}

The Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) is the central output of F-TAG. The supporting metrics in this section are organized around four operational questions an investigator needs answered for each candidate attack path.

\subsubsection*{Timeline Bounding}

\textbf{Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC).} Given a temporal path $p$ with $H$ hops and a set of $N$ observed evidence timestamps $\mathcal{E} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N\}$ mapped to time windows, the FWoC computes the time interval during which the attack could have occurred. It does so by intersecting the structural corridor (the TAC from Definition~3) with the evidence-bounded interval:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwoc}
\text{FWoC}(p) = \text{TAC}(p) \cap \left[\min(\mathcal{E}) - \delta - \delta_s,\ \max(\mathcal{E}) + \delta + \delta_s\right]
\end{equation}

Two correction terms appear in the equation. The first, $\delta$, is a clock-skew tolerance that absorbs NTP drift and timestamp uncertainty. The second, $\delta_s$, is a topology-aware small-sample correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_s}
\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)
\end{equation}

When evidence is sparse ($N < H$), $\delta_s$ widens the bounds to compensate for the fact that the observed earliest and latest timestamps naturally drift inward as fewer data points are available. When evidence is complete ($N \ge H$), $\delta_s = 0$ and the FWoC relies purely on the raw evidence bounds.

\textbf{Absolute Worst-Case Boundary of $\delta_s$.}\label{sec:worst-case-bounds} Consider a path with $H$ hops and $N$ evidence points; at most $H-N$ actions are unobserved, and in the worst case these occur entirely before the first or after the last observation. Since each action spans at most one window, expanding the bounds by $\delta_s = \max(0, H-N)$ windows in each direction guarantees the FWoC captures the true start and end, under the assumption that the attacker executed the path contiguously.

\textit{Contradiction Detection:} If $\text{FWoC}(p) = \emptyset$, the evidence timestamps are inconsistent with the structural corridor. This means the observed alerts could not have occurred along this path given the network's actual topology at those times. This directly signals a false-positive alert, a falsified timestamp, or an incorrect attack hypothesis. Static graph approaches cannot perform this check because they have no temporal reference frame.

\subsection{Topological Triage \& Scoping}\label{sec:network-metrics}

The metrics in Section~\ref{sec:path-metrics} evaluate individual attack paths. This section lifts those results to the network level to answer two practical questions: which hosts must the investigator examine first, and how far back in time should the investigation reach?

\subsubsection*{Host-Level Triage}

\textbf{Temporal Chokepoint Score} ($\chi$): The fraction of evidentially surviving candidate paths that pass through a given host $h$:
\begin{equation}
\chi(h) = \frac{|\{p \in \mathcal{P}_{surviving} : h \in V(p)\}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}|}
\end{equation}

A host with $\chi = 1.0$ lies on every surviving path. The attacker must have traversed it, making it the highest-priority target for the investigator. This produces a prioritized hit-list ranked by each host's criticality to the specific incident, rather than by abstract topological importance.

\textbf{Host Exposure Window}: The set of time windows during which a host was both vulnerable and reachable: $W_{exposure}(h) = \bigcup_{e \in E_{all}: h \in \{e.src, e.dst\}} \mathcal{W}(e)$.

\subsubsection*{Investigation Scoping}

\textbf{Temporal Search Radius} ($T_{min}$): The earliest time window across all backward-reachable paths to the victim node $j^*$: $T_{min}(j^*) = \min_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{*j^*}} w_0(p)$.

This tells the investigator exactly how far back to search through historical logs. Any events before $T_{min}$ are structurally irrelevant to the incident. Crucially, $T_{min}$ depends only on the graph topology, not on the evidence. Even when 75\% of evidence is missing, $T_{min}$ remains stable, serving as a guaranteed anchor for scoping the investigation.



\subsection{Anti-Forensics Confidence (\texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf})}\label{sec:afconf}

When F-TAG identifies a gap in the evidence (a hop with no corresponding log entry), a natural question arises: was this evidence lost through routine causes (log rotation, storage failure), or did the attacker deliberately wipe it?

The Anti-Forensics Confidence metric addresses this question using Bayesian reasoning. It is a secondary, exploratory metric compared to the deterministic bounds of the FWoC. However, it warrants careful validation because a false accusation of deliberate tampering can severely misdirect incident responders and unnecessarily escalate triage severity.

The approach extends prior Bayesian attack graph models~\cite{Frigault2017,Kazeminajafabadi2023} from proactive risk assessment to retroactive forensic analysis. For a missing-evidence gap $G$ at node $n$ with observed features $x$ (primarily the privilege level at the node):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:afconf}
AF_{conf}(G) = P(H_1 \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}
\end{equation}
where $H_1$ is the hypothesis that the evidence was deliberately wiped, and $H_0$ is the hypothesis that it was lost through benign causes.

\subsubsection{Calibrating the Likelihoods.}

The two likelihood terms in Eq.~\ref{eq:afconf} are grounded in domain knowledge about how log-wiping actually works in practice.

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_1)$: Deliberate Wiping Likelihood.} Modifying system log files (e.g., \texttt{/var/log/*}, Windows Event Logs, \texttt{auditd} records) requires root or administrative privileges. This operational reality provides a domain-informed likelihood: $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.75$ for root-privileged nodes and $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.25$ for non-root nodes. This grounding is stronger than tool-launch based approaches (e.g., Liu et al.~\cite{liu2012using}), because it reflects the fundamental OS-level access control model rather than relying on signatures of specific anti-forensic tools.

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_0)$: Benign Loss Likelihood.} This term is calibrated directly from the graph topology. It equals the base rate of root-level nodes across the entire Datalog state space $\mathcal{G}^a$:
\begin{equation}
P(x \mid H_0) = P_{root,H_0} = \frac{|\{n \in V_{all} : \text{priv}(n) = \text{root}\}|}{|V_{all}|}
\end{equation}

Because this base rate is computed from the specific topology under investigation, $AF_{conf}$ automatically adapts to different network architectures rather than relying on fixed assumptions about how many machines have root access.

\subsubsection{Formal Properties.}
We establish two properties characterizing the behavior of $AF_{conf}$.

\textit{Lemma 1 (Monotonicity under Uniform Base Rates):} When structural base rates are uniform across privilege levels, $AF_{conf}(G)$ is strictly non-decreasing with respect to the attacker's privilege level at the point of evidence loss.

\textit{Proof:} Let $x_{root}$ and $x_{user}$ represent root and user-level privileges. By our domain grounding, $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$. Holding the structural base rate equal ($P(x_{root} \mid H_0) = P(x_{user} \mid H_0) = c$), the posterior ratio $\frac{AF_{conf}(G_{root})}{AF_{conf}(G_{user})}$ simplifies via the monotonically increasing function $f(y) = \frac{a y}{a y + b}$. Since $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$, it follows that $AF_{conf}(G_{root}) > AF_{conf}(G_{user})$. $\square$

\textit{Boundary Condition (Root Saturation Inversion):} In networks saturated with root-privileged nodes ($p \to 1$), the base rate for non-root nodes approaches zero ($P(x_{user} \mid H_0) \to 0$). Here, missing evidence at a rare non-root node produces an artificially high $AF_{conf}$ score, inverting the expected ordering.

\textit{Lemma 2 (Root-Node Posterior Limit under Saturation):} As the network becomes saturated with root-level nodes, the $AF_{conf}$ score for root nodes drops strictly below the prior.

\textit{Proof:} Let $P(x \mid H_0) \to 1$. With operational setting $P(x \mid H_1) = \alpha = 0.75$, the limit is $\frac{0.75 P(H_1)}{0.75 P(H_1) + P(H_0)}$. Because $P(H_1) < 1$ and $P(H_0) > 0$, this is strictly less than the prior $P(H_1)$. The limit only approaches $P(H_1)$ if we assume deliberate wiping \textit{always} occurs at root nodes ($\alpha \to 1$). $\square$

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

To validate F-TAG, we employ two complementary configurations. \textbf{DARPA LLDOS (1.0 and 2.0.2)} provides historically verified multi-stage intrusions (distributed DDoS and stealthy linear exfiltration) to test temporal reconstruction, evidence degradation, and small-sample correction ($\delta_s$) against ground truth. We mapped the verified capability paths into F-TAGs and executed 100-trial Monte Carlo sweeps across four completeness levels (25\%--100\%) to test metric robustness under severe evidence loss. \textbf{MulVAL} generates scalable, industry-standard enterprise topologies to validate temporal topology construction, $AF_{conf}$ Bayesian calibration, and computational scalability.

\subsection{Scalable Topology Generation (MulVAL)}\label{sec:trackb}

To demonstrate that F-TAG scales to modern enterprise topologies with complex adversarial tactics, we employed the MulVAL logic engine~\cite{mulval}, an industry-standard tool that generates attack graphs from Datalog specifications of network configurations and vulnerability databases.

\subsubsection{Temporal Generation Engine and Formal Datalog Mapping.} 
We wrapped MulVAL in an automated temporal simulator that dynamically injects vulnerabilities across specific windows (simulating patch cycles) and runs iteratively to compile a unified F-TAG. To rigorously bind the static output to the temporal domain, the engine automatically maps the existence of prerequisites (e.g., \texttt{vulExists}, \texttt{netAccess}) to a subset of active windows. The resulting edge $E = (\text{Attacker}, \text{Host})$ inherits its active window set from the union of times these prerequisites are satisfied, $\mathcal{W}(E) = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau}\{t : \texttt{vulExists}_t \wedge \texttt{netAccess}_t\}$.
This ensures topological edges strictly adhere to physical reality at any given time $w$, resolving impossible static intersections proactively. Furthermore, MulVAL outputs Goal$\to$Precondition dependency edges; our engine reverses these into chronologically accurate Attacker$\to$Goal causal pathways, verified to preserve active window sets during transformation.

\subsubsection{Adversarial Dormancy Topology.}\label{sec:adversarial-dormancy} To empirically test the boundary condition of contiguous execution (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}), we generated a specialized 5-hop linear chain topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) via MulVAL. We extended the active vulnerability window across 21 windows (W8 to W28) and explicitly instantiated a dormant adversarial path: an early cluster of reconnaissance hops, an 18-window period of dormancy, and a final cluster of exfiltration hops. For this synthetic scenario, the \textit{true compromise interval} was defined as the span from the initial pivot establishment through the final exfiltration (W9 to W28), strictly mirroring the ``exploit through launch'' convention established for the LLDOS datasets.

\subsubsection{Static vs. Temporal Comparison.} Using a 6-node linear chain topology (built with the same generator used for the adversarial dormancy experiments introduced above, here configured with a narrower W8-W13 active window), we ran a direct comparison between a traditional static attack graph and our temporal attack graph. The topology restricts vulnerabilities to a 6-window span (W8-W13) across 20 total time windows. The static graph identified exactly 1 structural path to the target. The F-TAG expanded this into \textbf{252 distinct chronological corridors} (representing every valid temporal instantiation of the 5 attacker hops across the active windows), accurately mapping how the attack could span across time (Fig.~\ref{fig:static-vs-tag}).

\subsubsection{False-Positive Filtering Experiment.} We also ran a targeted false-positive filtering test to demonstrate how F-TAG handles alerts that violate temporal prerequisites. The full scenario and its results are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-scenarios}.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration of \texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf}}\label{sec:trackc}

To rigorously calibrate $AF_{conf}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:afconf}), we constructed a large-scale Monte Carlo injection framework. Over 10 random seeds and three prior settings ($P(H_1) \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$), we injected thousands of synthetic missing-evidence gaps into the MulVAL topology (evenly distributed across all 15 candidate paths to ensure a true topological average, $\sim$45 samples per class per seed) and separately into the LLDOS topologies, testing the same three distinct injection classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wipe-Class ($H_1$)}: Deliberate log-wiping biased toward high-privilege pivot nodes, weighted by both root status ($10\times$ weight for root nodes) and temporal chokepoint score $\chi$ (higher-$\chi$ nodes receive proportionally more wipe injections).
    \item \textbf{Random-Benign ($H_0$)}: Uniformly distributed evidence loss across all path nodes, simulating accidental data loss with no structural bias.
    \item \textbf{Rotation-Benign ($H_0$)}: Periodic gaps recurring at consistent nodes, simulating correlated log rotation schedules where the same machines consistently lose logs at regular intervals.
\end{enumerate}

The Rotation-Benign class tests the metric's robustness against structured administrative maintenance. Because periodic rotation affects a small, fixed subset of nodes globally, its intersection with any specific attack path is highly variable. This creates a structurally correlated, uncontrolled sample size of gaps that can either heavily target or completely miss the evaluated path, serving as a high-variance confound distinct from uniform noise.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Operational Context}

The three structural failure modes of static graphs (dormant attacker illusion, false-positive propagation, combinatorial intersection bugs) are each eliminated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}'s chronological constraint, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. Where Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence} build narratives bottom-up from observed logs and stall when logs are missing, F-TAG operates top-down, deriving structure from physical topology and injecting evidence as strict filters. This architectural inversion provides resilience via FWoC bounds, native contradiction detection, and a topology-derived $T_{min}$ anchor regardless of evidence loss. Path enumeration is bounded by $O(k_{max}^{H_{max}})$ but early termination keeps the effective out-degree small; metric computation (FWoC, $\chi$, $AF_{conf}$) then requires at most $O(|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}| \cdot H_{max})$, confirmed by the scalability results in Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability}. In practice, structural TAG generation runs proactively during vulnerability management cycles; upon incident detection, the reactive engine fuses SIEM alerts to compute FWoC, prioritize investigative hosts, and flag anti-forensic tampering.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

While F-TAG provides rigorous structural bounds on forensic data, several limitations constrain its current operational generalizability. First, our anti-forensics ground truth and $AF_{conf}$ discriminative thresholds were validated primarily on synthetic topologies (MulVAL), making live SIEM data verification across diverse enterprise architectures a necessary future step. Second, the $AF_{conf}$ model relies on a manually grounded, binary privilege distribution (root vs. non-root), which limits its discriminative power compared to granular access control models. Finally, while computational cost scales predictably, the combinatorial edge density in highly dense networks requires active hierarchical decomposition to maintain real-time triage performance.

The worst-case $\delta_s$ correction (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}) is proven under an assumption of contiguous execution, that unobserved hops occur at approximately one window of drift each. Under this assumption, soundness (the probability the true compromise falls within the computed FWoC) holds unconditionally, as confirmed empirically on both LLDOS datasets (100\% across all completeness levels). We stress-tested this assumption on a synthetic MulVAL topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) modeling a deliberately dormant APT with an 18-window gap between reconnaissance and final action. Under this adversarial dormancy pattern, soundness degrades from 0\% at minimal evidence ($N=1$) to 100\% at full evidence ($N=5$) (see Table~\ref{tab:dormant-apt}), demonstrating that the correction's guarantee is scoped to bounded inter-hop gaps rather than unconditional across arbitrary attacker dormancy. Practitioners investigating suspected long-dormancy campaigns should treat FWoC bounds with reduced confidence when evidence is sparse and gaps between observed evidence points are large relative to path length.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG), a framework fusing proactive structural risk assessment with reactive digital evidence. Our expanded Monte Carlo validation demonstrates that the Forensic Window of Compromise computes provable bounds on unobserved attacker actions, maintaining 100\% soundness across all evidence degradation levels on both real-world LLDOS benchmarks, under the correction's proven contiguous-execution assumption (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}). We further show this guarantee is bounded under deliberate long-dormancy adversarial behavior (Section~\ref{sec:adversarial-dormancy}). The topology-derived $T_{min}$ remains unconditionally stable regardless of evidence loss, serving as a fail-safe investigative anchor. F-TAG successfully detects temporally-dependent falsification scenarios where static methods fail, and the Anti-Forensics Confidence metric achieves a 0.0\% false-accusation rate at the operational threshold of 0.85, pending real-world validation. By shifting from bottom-up evidence reconstruction to top-down structural filtering, F-TAG provides a mathematically grounded approach to network investigation resilient to evidence degradation. All experimental code will be made publicly available upon publication to ensure reproducibility.
