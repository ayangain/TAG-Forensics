\title{F-TAG: Forensic Temporal Attack Graphs for Evidence-Resilient Network Investigation}

\author{Ayan Gain$^{1}$ \and Mridul Sankar Barik$^{1}$ \\
Jadavpur University, Kolkata, India \\
\texttt{ayan.gain2010@gmail.com, mridulsankar.barik@jadavpuruniversity.in}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Modern network forensics heavily relies on the reactive reconstruction of digital evidence, such as intrusion detection alerts and system logs. However, digital evidence is fundamentally fragile, as it is prone to accidental loss and vulnerable to deliberate anti-forensic tampering. Traditional Evidence Graphs attempt to reconstruct attack paths purely from observed logs, breaking down completely when evidence is missing. Furthermore, attempting to correlate alerts over legacy static attack graphs ignores the physical reality of evolving network topologies, leading to causality bugs and false positives. In this paper, we ask a fundamental question: \emph{Can a temporally evolving attack topology serve as a strict structural constraint for forensic reconstruction when evidence is incomplete or contradictory?} To answer this, we formalize the Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG). By injecting digital evidence as constraints upon the physical topology, F-TAG natively detects temporal contradictions. We derive the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) to compute provable bounds on unobserved attacker actions under a contiguous-execution assumption, and we formally characterize the limits of these guarantees under adversarial dormancy. We demonstrate the efficacy of F-TAG on the DARPA LLDOS 1.0 and 2.0.2 benchmarks and MulVAL-generated enterprise topologies, successfully recovering complete attack narratives despite severe evidence degradation.

\textbf{Keywords:} Network Forensics, Temporal Attack Graphs, Anti-Forensics, Bayesian Analysis
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Imagine an investigator responding to a breach whose only visible trace is a single IDS alert on the database; the upstream web server's logs are gone. Did the attacker exploit a vulnerability patched last week, or a different one entirely? Was the log wiped on purpose? Without answers, the investigation stalls~\cite{khan2022digital}. Two separate problems are tangled together here: the evidence itself may be missing or falsified, and even the evidence that survives must be checked against whether the attack it implies could ever have actually happened, given how the network changed over time.

Existing tools solve one problem or the other, never both. \emph{Evidence Graphs}~\cite{wang2008evidence} reconstruct causal chains directly from logs and alerts; they work well when evidence is complete, but the chain simply breaks wherever a log is wiped. \emph{Static Attack Graphs}~\cite{1004377,Lallie2020}, used to fill such gaps by correlating alerts against known vulnerability paths~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building}, solve the opposite problem: they represent every path the network has ever permitted, but collapse that history into one timeless snapshot with no notion of when each path was actually usable.

\textbf{Why a static attack graph is not enough.} A static attack graph answers a spatial question --- \emph{``can these capabilities be chained on this topology?''} --- while forensics needs an answer to a spatiotemporal one: \emph{``could this specific sequence of actions have happened in this order, given how the topology changed over time?''}

Consider a vulnerability patched on Tuesday. A static attack graph still shows that exploitation path as open on Wednesday, so if an IDS alert fires that day, the graph will happily accept a route through the already-closed vulnerability. The failure here is not that individual edges lack timestamps; it is that nothing enforces temporal consistency \emph{across the whole path}. Each hop might be individually plausible at some point in the network's history, yet the full sequence may never have been jointly possible: an algorithm that stitches an early edge (Week 1) to a late edge (Week 3) through a node patched in Week 2 produces a ``Frankenstein path'' --- assembled from pieces that were each valid in isolation, but never coexisted.

This absence of end-to-end temporal enforcement produces three distinct forensic failure modes. \textbf{Temporal Feasibility Errors} occur when the topology itself is internally inconsistent: an edge existed, but not at the moment the preceding hop required it. \textbf{Topology Transition Errors} occur when an enabling vulnerability disappears mid-attack, between one stage and the next. \textbf{Evidence Stitching Errors} occur one level up, at the evidence layer: individual alerts each match some static edge, but no single chronological sequence can accommodate all of them together. In every case, the underlying cause is the same --- the graph has no way to ask whether a \emph{sequence}, not just its individual steps, was ever physically realizable.

Temporal Attack Graphs (TAGs) resolve the first two failure modes structurally, by requiring that the window in which each hop is used never precede the window used by the hop before it. This is the key difference from simply stamping timestamps onto static edges: a TAG enforces the ordering constraint \emph{jointly across the entire path during traversal}, not edge by edge after the fact, so a Frankenstein path is never assembled in the first place. What a TAG alone cannot do is the third failure mode: it has no mechanism for taking in observed evidence and checking it against that structure, or for reasoning about what must have happened during a gap where evidence is missing. Forensic investigation needs precisely that --- a way to inject retrospective evidence as a constraint on a temporally consistent topology, and to draw sound conclusions when the evidence is incomplete or contradictory. This is the gap our framework closes; the relevant object for forensic correlation is therefore not a static attack path, but a temporally feasible attack corridor constrained by whatever evidence survives.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formalize temporal forensic reconstruction, injecting reactive evidence as strict constraints upon proactive, time-aware structural topologies.
    \item We derive the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC), establishing conditional soundness guarantees that bound unobserved attacker actions when evidence is missing.
    \item We demonstrate how this temporal structural constraint naturally enables contradiction detection, natively filtering false-positives and falsified evidence where static methods fail.
    \item We formally and empirically characterize the limits of these structural guarantees under adversarial dormancy.
    \item We introduce $AF_{conf}$, a secondary Bayesian metric for estimating the likelihood of deliberate anti-forensic wiping when evidence is missing.
    \item We present a large-scale experimental evaluation across DARPA LLDOS benchmarks and MulVAL enterprise topologies up to 100 hosts, demonstrating robust narrative recovery under severe evidence degradation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Attack Graphs and Alert Correlation}\label{sec:static-ag}

Attack Graphs proactively map how vulnerabilities can be chained across a network~\cite{Noel2018,Lallie2020}. Formally, a static attack graph $G = (E \cup C, R_{r} \cup R_{i})$ is a directed graph mapping exploits and security conditions as edges~\cite{6263942}. Alert correlation techniques match IDS alerts against these prerequisite-consequence relationships to trace attacks~\cite{10.1145/586110.586144,ning2004building,husak2019survey}. However, because $G$ carries no notion of time, it cannot model patches, configuration changes, or causal ordering constraints across windows. Alert correlation systems relying on these \emph{static} graphs can therefore accept alerts on closed paths or construct physically impossible backward-time causal chains --- the core limitation motivating F-TAG.

\subsection{Evidence Graphs and Network Forensics}

Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence,he2016network} structure observed digital evidence into graphs for investigative traversal, but stall when logs are missing. Approaches combining them with attack graphs typically use statistical models (Bayesian Networks, HMMs) to estimate missing events or detect falsifications~\cite{liu2012using,zhang2015detecting,Frigault2017}. F-TAG instead treats temporal topology as a hard physical boundary: a falsified timestamp produces an empty bounding intersection, an unambiguous structural contradiction rather than a probability estimate.
  
\subsection{Temporal and Dynamic Network Security}

Dynamic assessment methods recognize that temporal constraints fundamentally change path structures~\cite{Holme,Casteigts2012}, motivating temporal risk variance analysis~\cite{Awan2016}, dynamic attack surface evaluation~\cite{ENOCH2019102448}, and Temporal Attack Graph (TAG) formalisms~\cite{Gain2023}. 

In the proactive TAG framework~\cite{Gain2023}, the temporal topology and path structures are defined as follows:

\begin{definition}[\textbf{Temporal Attack Graph}]
\label{Def:Temporal Attack Graph}
A temporal attack graph\\ $\mathcal{G}^a(t_{min}, t_{max})$ is defined as a sequence of attack graphs $(G_0, G_1, \ldots, G_{\tau -1})$, such that:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $G_t=(V_t, E_t)$ is an attack graph $AG_t$ of the snapshot of the network taken at time instance $t$ 
	\item $\tau = ((t_{max}-t_{min})/w) = \mid \mathcal{G}^a(t_{min}, t_{max}) \mid $ is the number of graphs in the sequence, as well as the  number of time windows
	\item $w$ is the duration of each time window 
    \item \textit{$\bigl(E^{w} \cup C^{w} \bigl)$ is the vertex set and $\bigl( R_{r}^{w}\cup R_{i}^{w}\bigl)$ is the edge set of attack graph at time window $w$;} 
        \item \textit{$\left | E \cup C \right | = \sum_{w=1}^{\tau}\left | E^{w} \cup C^{w} \right |$ is the set of vertices across all time windows in the Temporal Attack Graph $\mathcal{G}^{a}$;}
        \item \textit{$\left | R_{r} \cup R_{i} \right | = \sum_{w=1}^{\tau}\left | R_{r}^{w} \cup R_{i}^{w} \right |$ is the set of edges across all time windows in the Temporal Attack Graph $\mathcal{G}^{a}$.}
\end{itemize}

\end{definition}


\begin{definition}[\textbf{Temporal Attack Path}]
\label{Def:Temporal Attack Path}
A temporal attack path, $p_{ij}^a = (n_0^{W_{b}}, \ldots, n_{\eta - 1}^{W_{k}})$, such that $b \leq k$, starting at node $i=n_0$ and finishing at node $j=n_{\eta-1}$ is defined over a Temporal Attack Graph $\mathcal{G}^a(t_{min}, t_{max})$ as a sequence of $\eta$ hops via distinct nodes $n_{h}^{W_{p}}$ at time window $W_p$ such that $b \leq p \leq k$, node $n_h$ is visited if and only if there is an edge between $n_{h-1}$ and $n_h$ at time window $W_{b}\leq W_p$.


\end{definition}
$Q^{a}_{ij}$ is the set of all temporal attack paths between nodes $i$ and $j$. If a temporal attack path between $i$ and $j$ does not exist, i.e., $Q^{a}_{ij} = \phi$ we can deduce that $(i,j)$ is a temporally disconnected node pair, and we set the distance $l^{a}_{ij} = \infty$

The function $D^{a}(p^a_{ij})$ returns the set of nodes in a given temporal attack path $p^a_{ij}$. To calculate the shortest temporal attack path length between two nodes $i$ and $j$, we define $d^{a}_{ij}$ as the minimum number of hops among all feasible paths $p^a_{ij}$ in the set $Q^{a}_{ij}$. Specifically, it can be expressed as the temporal shortest attack path length :
\begin{equation}
    d^{a}_{ij} = min(\left| D^{a}(p_{ij}^a) \right|),\forall\,  p_{ij}^a\, \epsilon \, Q^{a}_{ij}
    \label{eq9}
\end{equation}


where $Q^{a}_{ij}$ is the set of all possible attack paths between $i$ and $j$.
Since the shortest temporal attack path may not be unique, we define the set $S^{a}_{ij}$ of all shortest temporal attack paths from node $i$ to $j$ as:
\begin{equation}
    S^{a}_{ij} = \left \{ p_{ij}^a\,  \epsilon \,  Q^{a}_{ij} \, :\left ( D^{a}(p_{ij}^a) = d^{a}_{ij}\right ) \right \}
    \label{eq10}
\end{equation}

These models all remain forward-looking, assessing future risk without incorporating retrospective forensic evidence --- the gap F-TAG bridges by converting this proactive TAG framework into a retrospective forensic engine.

\subsection{Research Gap}

The related work above reveals a clear split. Evidence-based methods reconstruct what happened directly from observed events, but stall wherever intermediate evidence is missing. Attack-graph correlation fills such gaps using known prerequisite--consequence relationships, but represents those relationships as a timeless set of possible paths, with no way to check whether a candidate path was ever available as a coherent whole --- which is exactly why, as noted above, correlating alerts against such graphs can accept already-closed paths or stitch together physically impossible sequences.

Temporal Attack Graphs (TAGs) resolve this second problem structurally, representing feasibility as a function of network state over time. Unlike naive timestamped correlation, a TAG enforces the chronological constraint jointly across the whole path during traversal --- requiring the active-window sequence to be non-decreasing hop by hop --- rather than checking each edge in isolation after the fact. This is precisely the end-to-end check a static graph cannot perform. What TAGs do not do is incorporate the evidence itself: existing formulations are built to answer whether an attack was ever structurally possible, not to reconcile a temporally consistent topology against what alerts and logs actually report, particularly when that evidence is incomplete or contradictory. A gap therefore remains between \textbf{temporal structural feasibility}, which TAGs already solve, and \textbf{evidence-based reconstruction}, which they do not attempt.

\textbf{F-TAG} closes this remaining gap by treating the temporal topology as a hard structural constraint on forensic evidence (an assumption we make explicit and revisit in Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Rather than building the attack graph from the evidence itself, F-TAG first enumerates every temporally feasible attack corridor from the topology alone, then uses observed alerts and timestamps to constrain those corridors after the fact. This lets the framework reject evidence that is temporally impossible outright, while still bounding the unobserved portions of an otherwise-feasible attack through the Forensic Window of Compromise.

\section{Foundational Theory}\label{sec:theory}

Definition~3 and Algorithm~1 restate the proactive TAG formalism of~\cite{Gain2023} for self-containment, while Definitions~1--2 formally introduce the F-TAG structures. Sections~\ref{sec:methodology}--\ref{sec:evaluation} present this paper's original contributions---FWoC, $AF_{conf}$, falsification detection, and topological triage---built atop this substrate. The foundation of F-TAG relies on rigorous complex network theory to avoid state-space explosion and causal ambiguity.

\subsection{Definition 1: Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG)}\label{sec:def1}

The F-TAG engine executes a \emph{Reconstruction Phase} to collapse the discrete snapshots of a proactive TAG into a unified mathematical structure where each edge $e$ carries a set of \emph{active windows} $\mathcal{W}(e) \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, \tau-1\}$. This representation captures the physical reality that network edges (vulnerability exploitation paths) appear and disappear over time as configurations change, patches are applied, and firewall rules are modified. The unified representation enables efficient algorithmic traversal without redundant duplication of shared topology across windows.

\textbf{Definition 1.} Formally, the reconstructed Forensic Temporal Attack Graph (F-TAG) is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}^a_{unified} = \left(V_{all},\ E_{all},\ \mathcal{W}\right)
\end{equation}
where $V_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} V_t$, $E_{all} = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau-1} E_t$, and $\mathcal{W}: E_{all} \to 2^{\{0,\dots,\tau-1\}}$ maps each edge to the set of time windows in which it physically exists.

\subsection{Definition 2: Forensic Temporal Attack Path}\label{sec:def2}

\textbf{Definition 2.} Operating over the unified structure, a Forensic Temporal Attack Path $p^f_{ij}$ from node $i$ to node $j$ is a sequence of hops $\langle (e_0, w_0), (e_1, w_1), \dots, (e_{\eta-1}, w_{\eta-1}) \rangle$ satisfying three strict constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Existence Constraint:} Each edge $e_k$ is traversed at window $w_k \in \mathcal{W}(e_k)$, ensuring the exploitation path physically existed at the time it was used.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Constraint:} The window sequence is strictly non-decreasing: $w_0 \le w_1 \le \dots \le w_{\eta-1}$, ensuring the attacker cannot travel backward in time.
    \item \textbf{Evidence Constraint:} The temporal bounding interval of the sequence must not yield an empty intersection when constrained by retrospective observed evidence timestamps.
\end{enumerate}

By evaluating these constraints against the unified $\mathcal{W}$ mapping rather than discrete snapshots, the chronological constraint natively prevents backward-time causal loops while the evidence constraint natively rejects historically impossible narratives. This ensures that every enumerated path represents a physically realizable attack sequence consistent with forensic observation.

\subsection{Definition 3: Temporal Attack Corridor}\label{sec:tac}

\textbf{Definition 3.} For a Forensic Temporal Attack Path $p^f_{ij}$ comprising $\eta$ hops, the Temporal Attack Corridor (TAC) is defined as the continuous temporal bounding interval containing the entire attack sequence:
\begin{equation}
\text{TAC}(p^f_{ij}) = [w_0(p^f_{ij}),\ w_{\eta-1}(p^f_{ij})]
\end{equation}
where $w_0(p^f_{ij})$ represents the time window in which the initial hop (e.g., initial compromise at node $i$) was executed, and $w_{\eta-1}(p^f_{ij})$ represents the time window of the final hop (e.g., achieving the objective at node $j$).

The TAC encapsulates the full structural lifespan of a specific attack narrative. The corridor structurally binds the earliest possible start and latest possible end points of the path as dictated by the physical topology. As we will see in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, any retrospective evidence that falls outside the boundaries of this structurally enforced corridor signals an unambiguous contradiction, natively highlighting either a false positive alert or deliberate anti-forensic tampering.

\subsection{Path Enumeration Algorithm}\label{sec:enumeration}

The temporal path enumeration follows a depth-first search (Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}). The chronological pruning at Line 14 restricts traversal to $w \ge w_{min}$, preventing the generation of impossible ``Frankenstein'' paths stitched across non-overlapping vulnerability windows. While traditional correlation relies on local prerequisite-consequence matching without global temporal consistency, one could theoretically achieve correctness by enumerating all paths in a static graph and filtering post-hoc. However, this is combinatorially intractable. Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate} integrates temporal constraints directly into the traversal, providing an efficient mechanism without sacrificing correctness.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Temporal Path Enumeration ($Q^a_{ij}$)}
\label{alg:enumerate}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE F-TAG $\mathcal{G}^a$, source node $i$, target node $j$, max hops $H_{max}$
\ENSURE Set of all valid temporal attack paths $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \emptyset$
\STATE $\text{Stack} \gets \{(i, 0, \langle\rangle)\}$ \COMMENT{(node, min\_window, path)}
\WHILE{Stack $\neq \emptyset$}
    \STATE $(n_{curr}, w_{min}, \text{path}) \gets \text{Stack.pop()}$
    \IF{$n_{curr} = j$ \AND $|\text{path}| > 0$}
        \STATE $\mathcal{P}_{ij} \gets \mathcal{P}_{ij} \cup \{\text{TemporalPath(path)}\}$
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$|\text{path}| \ge H_{max}$}
        \STATE \textbf{continue}
    \ENDIF
    \FORALL{edges $e$ from $n_{curr}$}
        \FORALL{$w \in \mathcal{W}(e)$ where $w \ge w_{min}$}
            \STATE Stack.push$(e.dst, w, \text{path} \cup \{(e, w)\})$
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Completeness of Path Enumeration.}
We formally prove that Algorithm 1 is complete, i.e., it discovers all valid temporal attack paths without missing any structurally feasible corridors.

\textit{Theorem 1 (Completeness of Temporal Enumeration):} Let $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$ be the set of all paths from $i$ to $j$ returned by Algorithm 1. If there exists a temporal path $p^*$ from $i$ to $j$ satisfying the non-decreasing chronological constraints of Definition 2 with length $|p^*| \le H_{max}$, then $p^* \in \mathcal{P}_{ij}$.

\textit{Proof (sketch):}
Suppose a valid path $p^* = ((e_1,w_1),\dots,(e_n,w_n))$ with $n \le H_{max}$ is not returned. Since the DFS explores all outgoing edges up to depth $H_{max}$ (with revisits permitted), the only mechanism for missing $p^*$ is if the chronological filter at Line~12 prunes some edge $(e_k,w_k)$, meaning the algorithm holds $w_{min} > w_k$ at that step. But the algorithm sets $w_{min} = w_{k-1}$ on arrival at $e_k.\text{src}$, so $w_{min} > w_k$ implies $w_{k-1} > w_k$, contradicting the non-decreasing constraint that $p^*$ must satisfy by Definition~2. Therefore no valid edge is ever pruned, and $p^*$ must be discovered. $\square$

\section{The F-TAG Framework}\label{sec:methodology}

Section~\ref{sec:theory} formalized the proactive temporal graph theory that F-TAG inherits from prior TAG research. This section introduces the forensic engine itself: how F-TAG consumes observed evidence (IDS alerts, firewall logs, system timestamps) and produces actionable investigative outputs.

The framework is organized around three layers. First, \emph{path-level forensic bounds} (Section~\ref{sec:path-metrics}) answer questions about individual attack paths: how wide is the window of compromise, how well does the evidence fit, and how fast did the attacker move? Second, \emph{topological triage} (Section~\ref{sec:network-metrics}) aggregates those path-level results into a network-wide view, identifying which hosts the investigator should examine first and how far back in time to search. Third, the \emph{Anti-Forensics Confidence} metric (Section~\ref{sec:afconf}) adds a probabilistic layer that flags evidence gaps that are likely caused by deliberate log-wiping rather than routine loss.

\subsection{Path-Level Forensic Bounds}\label{sec:path-metrics}

The Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) is the central output of F-TAG. The supporting metrics in this section are organized around four operational questions an investigator needs answered for each candidate attack path.

\textbf{Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC).} Given a temporal path $p$ with $H$ hops and a set of $N$ observed evidence timestamps $\mathcal{E} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N\}$ mapped to time windows, the FWoC computes the time interval during which the attack could have occurred. It does so by intersecting the structural corridor (the TAC from Definition~3) with the evidence-bounded interval:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwoc}
\text{FWoC}(p) = \text{TAC}(p) \cap \left[\min(\mathcal{E}) - \delta - \delta_s,\ \max(\mathcal{E}) + \delta + \delta_s\right]
\end{equation}

Two correction terms appear. The first, $\delta$, is a clock-skew tolerance absorbing NTP drift and timestamp uncertainty. The second is a topology-aware small-sample correction, $\delta_s = \max(0, H - N)$. When evidence is sparse ($N < H$), $\delta_s$ widens the bounds to compensate for observed timestamps drifting inward as data points decrease. When complete ($N \ge H$), $\delta_s = 0$ and the FWoC relies purely on raw evidence bounds.

\textbf{Absolute Worst-Case Boundary of $\delta_s$.}\label{sec:worst-case-bounds} Consider a path with $H$ hops and $N$ evidence points; at most $H-N$ actions are unobserved, and in the worst case these occur entirely before the first or after the last observation. Since each action spans at most one window, expanding the bounds by $\delta_s = \max(0, H-N)$ windows in each direction guarantees the FWoC captures the true start and end, under the assumption that the attacker executed the path contiguously.

\textit{Contradiction Detection:} If $\text{FWoC}(p) = \emptyset$, the evidence timestamps are inconsistent with the structural corridor. This means the observed alerts could not have occurred along this path given the network's actual topology at those times. This directly signals a false-positive alert, a falsified timestamp, or an incorrect attack hypothesis. Static graph approaches cannot perform this check because they have no temporal reference frame.

\subsection{Topological Triage \& Scoping}\label{sec:network-metrics}

The metrics in Section~\ref{sec:path-metrics} evaluate individual attack paths. This section lifts those results to the network level to answer two practical questions: which hosts must the investigator examine first, and how far back in time should the investigation reach?

\textbf{Temporal Chokepoint Score} ($\chi$): The fraction of evidentially surviving candidate paths that pass through a given host $h$:
\begin{equation}
\chi(h) = \frac{|\{p \in \mathcal{P}_{surviving} : h \in V(p)\}|}{|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}|}
\end{equation}

A host with $\chi = 1.0$ lies on every surviving path. The attacker must have traversed it, making it the highest-priority target for the investigator. This produces a prioritized hit-list ranked by each host's criticality to the specific incident, rather than by abstract topological importance.

\textbf{Host Exposure Window}: The set of time windows during which a host was both vulnerable and reachable: $W_{exposure}(h) = \bigcup_{e \in E_{all}: h \in \{e.src, e.dst\}} \mathcal{W}(e)$.

\textbf{Temporal Search Radius} ($T_{min}$): The earliest time window across all backward-reachable paths to the victim node $j^*$: $T_{min}(j^*) = \min_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{*j^*}} w_0(p)$.

This tells the investigator exactly how far back to search through historical logs. Any events before $T_{min}$ are structurally irrelevant to the incident. Crucially, $T_{min}$ depends only on the graph topology, not on the evidence. Even when 75\% of evidence is missing, $T_{min}$ remains stable, serving as a guaranteed anchor for scoping the investigation.

\subsection{Anti-Forensics Confidence (\texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf})}\label{sec:afconf}

When F-TAG identifies a gap in the evidence (a hop with no corresponding log entry), a natural question arises: was this evidence lost through routine causes (log rotation, storage failure), or did the attacker deliberately wipe it?

The Anti-Forensics Confidence metric addresses this question using Bayesian reasoning. It is a secondary, exploratory metric compared to the deterministic bounds of the FWoC. However, it warrants careful validation because a false accusation of deliberate tampering can severely misdirect incident responders and unnecessarily escalate triage severity.

The approach extends prior Bayesian attack graph models~\cite{Frigault2017,Kazeminajafabadi2023} from proactive risk assessment to retroactive forensic analysis. For a missing-evidence gap $G$ at node $n$ with observed features $x$ (primarily the privilege level at the node):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:afconf}
AF_{conf}(G) = P(H_1 \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)\,P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}
\end{equation}
where $H_1$ is the hypothesis that the evidence was deliberately wiped, and $H_0$ is the hypothesis that it was lost through benign causes.

\subsubsection{Calibrating the Likelihoods.}

The two likelihood terms in Eq.~\ref{eq:afconf} are grounded in domain knowledge about how log-wiping actually works in practice.

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_1)$: Deliberate Wiping Likelihood.} Modifying system log files (e.g., \texttt{/var/log/*}, Windows Event Logs, \texttt{auditd} records) requires root or administrative privileges. This operational reality provides a domain-informed likelihood: $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.75$ for root-privileged nodes and $P(x \mid H_1) = 0.25$ for non-root nodes. We hypothesize this provides a more robust signal than tool-launch based approaches (e.g., Liu et al.~\cite{liu2012using}), because it reflects the fundamental OS-level access control model rather than relying on signatures of specific anti-forensic tools.

\textbf{$P(x \mid H_0)$: Benign Loss Likelihood.} This term is calibrated directly from the graph topology. It equals the base rate of root-level nodes across the entire Datalog state space $\mathcal{G}^a$:
\begin{equation}
P(x \mid H_0) = P_{root,H_0} = \frac{|\{n \in V_{all} : \text{priv}(n) = \text{root}\}|}{|V_{all}|}
\end{equation}

Because this base rate is computed from the specific topology under investigation, $AF_{conf}$ automatically adapts to different network architectures rather than relying on fixed assumptions about how many machines have root access.

\subsubsection{Formal Properties.}
We establish two properties characterizing the behavior of $AF_{conf}$.

\begin{lemma}[Monotonicity under Uniform Base Rates]
When structural base rates are uniform across privilege levels, $AF_{conf}(G)$ is strictly non-decreasing with respect to the attacker's privilege level at the point of evidence loss.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Let $x_{root}$ and $x_{user}$ represent root and user-level privileges. By our domain grounding, $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$. Holding the structural base rate equal ($P(x_{root} \mid H_0) = P(x_{user} \mid H_0) = c$), the posterior ratio $\frac{AF_{conf}(G_{root})}{AF_{conf}(G_{user})}$ simplifies via the monotonically increasing function $f(y) = \frac{a y}{a y + b}$. Since $P(x_{root} \mid H_1) > P(x_{user} \mid H_1)$, it follows that $AF_{conf}(G_{root}) > AF_{conf}(G_{user})$. $\square$

\textit{Boundary Condition (Root Saturation Inversion):} In networks saturated with root-privileged nodes ($p \to 1$), the base rate for non-root nodes approaches zero ($P(x_{user} \mid H_0) \to 0$). Here, missing evidence at a rare non-root node produces an artificially high $AF_{conf}$ score, inverting the expected ordering.

\begin{lemma}[Root-Node Posterior Limit under Saturation]
As the network becomes saturated with root-level nodes, the $AF_{conf}$ score for root nodes drops strictly below the prior.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Let $P(x \mid H_0) \to 1$. With operational setting $P(x \mid H_1) = \alpha = 0.75$, the limit is $\frac{0.75 P(H_1)}{0.75 P(H_1) + P(H_0)}$. Because $P(H_1) < 1$ and $P(H_0) > 0$, this is strictly less than the prior $P(H_1)$. The limit only approaches $P(H_1)$ if we assume deliberate wiping \textit{always} occurs at root nodes ($\alpha \to 1$). $\square$

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

To validate F-TAG, we employ two complementary configurations. \textbf{DARPA LLDOS (1.0 and 2.0.2)} provides historically verified multi-stage intrusions (distributed DDoS and stealthy linear exfiltration) to test temporal reconstruction, evidence degradation, and small-sample correction ($\delta_s$) against ground truth. We mapped the verified capability paths into F-TAGs and executed 100-trial Monte Carlo sweeps across four completeness levels (25\%--100\%) to test metric robustness under severe evidence loss. \textbf{MulVAL} generates scalable, industry-standard enterprise topologies to validate temporal topology construction, $AF_{conf}$ Bayesian calibration, and computational scalability.

\subsection{Scalable Topology Generation (MulVAL)}\label{sec:trackb}

To demonstrate that F-TAG scales to modern enterprise topologies with complex adversarial tactics, we employed the MulVAL logic engine~\cite{mulval}, an industry-standard tool that generates attack graphs from Datalog specifications of network configurations and vulnerability databases.

\subsubsection{Temporal Generation Engine and Formal Datalog Mapping.} 
We wrapped MulVAL in a temporal simulator that injects vulnerabilities across specific windows (simulating patch cycles) to compile a unified F-TAG. To bind the static output to the temporal domain, the engine maps prerequisites (\texttt{vulExists}, \texttt{netAccess}) to active windows. The resulting edge $E = (\text{Attacker}, \text{Host})$ inherits its active window set from the union of times these prerequisites are satisfied: $\mathcal{W}(E) = \bigcup_{t=0}^{\tau} \{t : \texttt{vulExists}_t \wedge \texttt{netAccess}_t\}$. This ensures edges strictly adhere to physical reality, resolving static intersections proactively. Finally, MulVAL's Goal$\to$Precondition dependency edges are reversed into chronologically accurate Attacker$\to$Goal pathways.

\subsubsection{Adversarial Dormancy Topology.}\label{sec:adversarial-dormancy} To empirically test the boundary condition of contiguous execution (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}), we generated a specialized 5-hop linear chain topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) via MulVAL. We extended the active vulnerability window across 21 windows (W8 to W28) and explicitly instantiated a dormant adversarial path: an early cluster of reconnaissance hops, an 18-window period of dormancy, and a final cluster of exfiltration hops. For this synthetic scenario, the \textit{true compromise interval} was defined as the span from the initial pivot establishment through the final exfiltration (W9 to W28), strictly mirroring the ``exploit through launch'' convention established for the LLDOS datasets.

\subsubsection{Static vs. Temporal Comparison.} Using a 6-node chain topology (from the same generator as above, but with a narrower W8-W13 active window), we compared a static attack graph to F-TAG. The topology restricts vulnerabilities to a 6-window span across 20 total windows. The static graph identified exactly 1 structural path to the target. F-TAG expanded this into 252 distinct chronological corridors (representing every valid temporal instantiation of the 5 attacker hops), accurately mapping how the attack could span across time (Fig.~\ref{fig:static-vs-tag}).

\subsubsection{False-Positive Filtering Experiment.} We also ran a targeted false-positive filtering test to demonstrate how F-TAG handles alerts that violate temporal prerequisites. The full scenario and its results are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-scenarios}.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration of \texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf}}\label{sec:trackc}

To rigorously calibrate $AF_{conf}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:afconf}), we constructed a large-scale Monte Carlo injection framework. Over 10 random seeds and three prior settings ($P(H_1) \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.30\}$), we injected thousands of synthetic missing-evidence gaps into the MulVAL topology (evenly distributed across all 15 candidate paths to ensure a true topological average, $\sim$45 samples per class per seed) and separately into the LLDOS topologies, testing the same three distinct injection classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wipe-Class ($H_1$)}: Deliberate log-wiping biased toward high-privilege pivot nodes, weighted by both root status ($10\times$ weight for root nodes) and temporal chokepoint score $\chi$ (higher-$\chi$ nodes receive proportionally more wipe injections).
    \item \textbf{Random-Benign ($H_0$)}: Uniformly distributed evidence loss across all path nodes, simulating accidental data loss with no structural bias.
    \item \textbf{Rotation-Benign ($H_0$)}: Periodic gaps recurring at consistent nodes, simulating correlated log rotation schedules where the same machines consistently lose logs at regular intervals.
\end{enumerate}

The Rotation-Benign class tests robustness against structured administrative maintenance; because periodic rotation affects a small, fixed subset of nodes globally, its intersection with any specific path is highly variable, serving as a high-variance confound distinct from uniform noise (analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:results-afconf}).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$AF_{conf}$}{AF\_conf} Bayesian Calibration}\label{sec:results-afconf}

Fig.~\ref{fig:fwoc-afconf}(b) presents the discriminative power of $AF_{conf}$ across prior settings on the MulVAL topology.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Figures/fig_fwoc_afconf.png}
\caption{(a) FWoC soundness and tightness under evidence loss for LLDOS (illustrating structural saturation). (b) $AF_{conf}$ maintains stable discriminative power across all prior settings, well above the random-chance baseline of 0.50.}
\label{fig:fwoc-afconf}
\end{figure}

\begin{lemma}[Prior-Invariance of Ranking]\label{lemma:prior-invariance}
For fixed $x \in \{\text{root}, \text{non-root}\}$, the posterior $AF_{conf}(x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)P(H_1)}{P(x \mid H_1)P(H_1) + P(x \mid H_0)P(H_0)}$ is a strictly increasing function of the likelihood ratio $LR(x) = \frac{P(x \mid H_1)}{P(x \mid H_0)}$ for any $P(H_1) \in (0,1)$. Since the set of achievable $LR$ values (and hence the induced ranking over samples) is independent of $P(H_1)$, the ROC curve --- and therefore AUC --- is invariant to the choice of prior.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof:} Write $AF_{conf}(x) = \frac{LR(x)P(H_1)}{LR(x)P(H_1) + (1-P(H_1))}$, which is strictly increasing in $LR(x)$ for any fixed $P(H_1) \in (0,1)$ since $\frac{\partial AF_{conf}}{\partial LR} = \frac{P(H_1)(1-P(H_1))}{(\cdot)^2} > 0$. As $LR(x)$ does not depend on $P(H_1)$, the ordering of $AF_{conf}$ across the two values of $x$ is preserved for every $P(H_1)$, so the ROC curve (which depends only on this ordering) is unchanged. $\square$

\subsubsection{Baseline Discriminative Power.} Because the current privilege model is binary (root/non-root), the ROC curve and its resulting AUC are analytic, deterministic consequences of the chosen likelihoods ($0.75 / 0.25$) and the topological base rates, rather than learned parameters. Across all three prior settings on the unpruned MulVAL topology, the AUC evaluates to 0.801 for Wipe vs. Random classification. This invariance follows analytically from Lemma~\ref{lemma:prior-invariance}; our Monte Carlo sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:fwoc-afconf}b) empirically confirms it holds numerically across three prior settings. Because $AF_{conf}$ takes only two distinct values (root/non-root), the ROC curve reduces to a single operating point, and AUC is the trapezoidal area under that point; the reported confidence interval reflects sampling variance in which nodes are drawn into each injection class across seeds, not variance in the score function itself.

\textit{Note on Topological Pruning:} This calibration intentionally used the raw, unpruned MulVAL topology, since the aggressive pruning applied for scalability (Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability}) collapses privilege variance into uniform \texttt{execCode} transitions, on which $AF_{conf}$'s signal would degrade.

\subsubsection{Rotation-Benign Variance.} When aggregated across all paths, Wipe vs. Rotation yielded an average AUC of 0.800 (95\% CI: [0.742, 0.858]), effectively identical to Random. Structured periodic rotation lands on root-privilege nodes at a rate mathematically equivalent to uniform noise on average (25.4\% vs. 25.2\%), but the far wider CI ([13.9\%, 37.0\%] vs. [24.6\%, 25.8\%]) reflects the fact that rotation's fixed global node subset intersects any single path unevenly.

Two single-run scenarios illustrate the logic: moving the attack origin internally dropped $\chi$ for perimeter nodes to 0.0 and promoted true entry points with no equation changes. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation and Operational Context}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:enumerate}'s constraint eliminates static graphs' three structural failure modes (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). Where Evidence Graphs~\cite{wang2008evidence} build narratives bottom-up and stall without logs, F-TAG operates top-down, injecting evidence as strict filters over a fixed topology --- providing resilience via FWoC bounds, native contradiction detection, and a $T_{min}$ anchor regardless of evidence loss. Path enumeration is bounded by $O(k_{max}^{H_{max}})$, but early termination keeps effective out-degree small; metric computation takes at most $O(|\mathcal{P}_{surviving}| \cdot H_{max})$ (Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability}). In practice, TAG generation runs proactively during vulnerability management, and the reactive engine fuses SIEM alerts on demand to compute FWoC, prioritize hosts, and flag tampering.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

While F-TAG provides rigorous structural bounds on forensic data, several limitations constrain its current operational generalizability. First, our anti-forensics ground truth and $AF_{conf}$ discriminative thresholds were validated primarily on synthetic topologies (MulVAL), making live SIEM data verification across diverse enterprise architectures a necessary future step. Second, the $AF_{conf}$ model relies on a manually grounded, binary privilege distribution (root vs. non-root), which limits its discriminative power compared to granular access control models. Third, while computational cost scales predictably, the combinatorial edge density in highly dense networks requires active hierarchical decomposition (e.g., aggregating subnets for topologies substantially larger than those tested here) to maintain real-time triage performance. Finally, F-TAG assumes the topology is a tamper-resistant ground truth. If network configuration or scan data resides on the compromised infrastructure itself, this out-of-band trust assumption breaks down, allowing adversaries to falsify both evidence and the structural reference frame.

The worst-case $\delta_s$ correction (Section~\ref{sec:worst-case-bounds}) is proven under an assumption of contiguous execution: unobserved hops occur at approximately one window of drift each. Under this assumption, soundness holds unconditionally, as confirmed on both LLDOS datasets (100\% across all completeness levels). We stress-tested this on a synthetic MulVAL topology ($H=5, \tau=30$) modeling a dormant APT with an 18-window gap between reconnaissance and final action; soundness degrades from 0\% at minimal evidence ($N=1$) to 100\% at full evidence ($N=5$) (Table~\ref{tab:dormant-apt}), showing the guarantee is scoped to bounded inter-hop gaps, not unconditional across arbitrary dormancy. Practitioners investigating suspected long-dormancy campaigns should treat FWoC bounds with reduced confidence when evidence is sparse and gaps are large relative to path length.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We answered the core question of whether a temporally evolving attack topology can serve as a structural constraint for forensic reconstruction. By formalizing the F-TAG framework, we demonstrated that fusing reactive evidence with proactive topologies provides robust narrative recovery. We derived the Forensic Window of Compromise (FWoC) to establish conditional soundness guarantees on unobserved actions (under a contiguous-execution assumption), and proved that this strict temporal enforcement naturally filters causality bugs and falsified timestamps. We characterized the limits of these bounds under long-dormancy adversarial behavior, and validated the framework extensively across LLDOS benchmarks and scaled enterprise topologies. (Secondary explorations into Bayesian log-wiping probabilities achieved a 0.0\% false-accusation rate, pending real-world validation). All experimental code will be made publicly available upon publication.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future-work}

Future work will focus on multi-level privilege modeling (e.g., SELinux) to improve $AF_{conf}$ discriminative power. Additionally, while the worst-case bound correctness was validated here under $H \approx \tau$, future experiments will empirically demonstrate FWoC narrowing under the $H \ll \tau$ regime (short attack paths embedded in long capture windows). Operational deployment necessitates cross-topology testing and calibration against live SIEM data with verified anti-forensic ground truth. Finally, integrating F-TAG with live SIEM pipelines would allow the FWoC to dynamically narrow during active incident triage.
